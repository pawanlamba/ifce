Chapter 4: Technical and Legal Principles in Insurance


Topic 1: The Principle of Utmost Good Faith (Uberrimae Fidei)

  • The Core Concept: A Higher Standard of Honesty

    • In most commercial contracts, the governing principle is caveat emptor, which means "let the buyer beware." It's up to each party to look out for their own interests and ask the right questions.

    • Insurance contracts are different. They are governed by a much stricter principle called Utmost Good Faith. This requires both the insurer and the person applying for insurance (the insured) to be completely honest and to voluntarily disclose all relevant information, even if not specifically asked.

    • The reason for this higher standard is that one party (the insured) knows all the facts about the risk being insured, while the other party (the insurer) must rely on the truthfulness of that information to decide whether to accept the risk and at what price.

  • The Duty of Disclosure and Material Facts

    • The core of Utmost Good Faith is the duty to disclose all material facts.

    • Definition of a Material Fact: A material fact is any piece of information that would influence the judgment of a prudent insurer in deciding whether to accept the risk, and if so, on what terms and at what premium.

    • The general rule for the insured is: If you are in doubt about whether something is relevant, disclose it.

    • Examples of Material Facts:

      • Motor Insurance: The driver's age, their accident history, the purpose for which the vehicle is used (e.g., personal use vs. business delivery), and any modifications made to the car's engine.

      • Fire Insurance: The type of construction materials used in the building (e.g., wood vs. concrete), what the building is used for (e.g., a quiet office vs. a chemical storage warehouse), and the nature of the properties next door.

      • Personal Accident Insurance: The applicant's health status and any pre-existing conditions that might increase the likelihood of an accident or injury.

  • What Does NOT Need to Be Disclosed?

    • There are some facts that an applicant is not required to disclose:

      • Facts of Law: Everyone is presumed to know the law.

      • Facts of Public or Common Knowledge: Information that is widely known, such as the location of a known flood zone or that a country is at war.

      • Facts that Lessen the Risk: For example, that you have installed a new, state-of-the-art sprinkler and alarm system.

      • Facts that the Insurer has Waived: If an applicant leaves a question on the proposal form blank and the insurer issues the policy without following up, they have waived their right to that information.

  • Consequences of a Breach

    • A breach of Utmost Good Faith can occur through:

      • Non-disclosure: Failing to volunteer a material fact.

      • Misrepresentation: Providing incorrect or misleading information. A deliberate misrepresentation is considered fraud.

    • If a breach is discovered (usually at the time of a claim), the injured party (the insurer) has several options:

      • Void the contract from the beginning: This means treating the policy as if it never existed and refusing to pay the claim. Any premiums paid may be returned.

      • Charge an additional premium: The insurer might agree to pay the claim but charge the extra premium they would have required if they had known the true facts from the start.

      • Impose different terms: The insurer might apply a special condition or a higher deductible that would have been on the policy had the facts been known.

  • When Does the Duty Apply?

    • The duty of Utmost Good Faith applies at the beginning of negotiations and continues until the contract is finalized. It then revives at renewal, because a renewal is legally a new contract. The insured must disclose any material changes that have occurred during the previous year.

Topic 2: The Principle of Insurable Interest

  • The Core Concept: The Legal Right to Insure

    • A person cannot insure just anything or anyone. They must have an insurable interest in the subject matter of the insurance.

    • Definition: Insurable interest is the legal right to insure, which arises from a recognized financial relationship between the insured and the subject matter of the insurance.

    • In simple terms, you must stand to suffer a direct financial loss if the event you are insuring against occurs. You must benefit from the item's safety and be prejudiced by its loss.

    • This principle prevents insurance from being used as a form of gambling and reduces the moral hazard of someone intentionally destroying property to collect insurance money.

  • How Insurable Interest is Created

    • The most common ways to have an insurable interest are:

      • Ownership: You have an insurable interest in your own house, car, and other property.

      • Legal Liability: A dry cleaner has an insurable interest in the clothes left in their care because they are legally responsible for them. A tenant has an insurable interest in a rented apartment because they are liable for damages under the lease.

      • Contract: A bank (mortgagee) that has provided a loan for a house has an insurable interest in that house because its destruction would jeopardize their financial security.

  • When Must Insurable Interest Exist?

    • The timing for when insurable interest must be present varies by the type of insurance:

      • General Insurance (Property, Motor, Liability): Insurable interest must exist both at the time the policy is taken out AND at the time of the loss.

        • Example: You cannot buy fire insurance on your neighbor's house (no interest at inception). If you sell your car, you cannot claim for an accident that happens after the sale is complete (no interest at the time of loss).

      • Life (Protection & Savings) Insurance: Insurable interest only needs to exist at the time the policy is taken out.

        • Example: A business partner can take out a life insurance policy on their partner. If the partnership later dissolves, the policy can remain valid, and the proceeds will be paid upon the former partner's death.

      • Marine Insurance: Due to the nature of international trade where goods can be bought and sold while in transit, insurable interest only needs to exist at the time of the loss.

Topic 3: The Principle of Indemnity

  • The Core Concept: No Profit, No Loss

    • The principle of indemnity is considered the foundation of most insurance contracts.

    • Definition: Indemnity means that the purpose of insurance is to put the insured back in the same financial position they were in immediately before the loss occurred. The insured should be neither better off nor worse off.

    • This principle ensures that insurance is used for protection, not for profit.

  • Contracts That Are NOT Indemnity Contracts

    • It is impossible to place a precise financial value on a human life or a limb. Therefore, Life (Protection & Savings) insurance and Personal Accident insurance are not contracts of indemnity. They pay a pre-agreed sum of money in the event of death or injury, regardless of the actual financial loss.

  • Measuring the Indemnity

    • To achieve a fair indemnity, the value of any lost or damaged item is assessed at the time and place of the loss.

    • This means deductions are often made for wear and tear and depreciation.

      • Example: Ali's 2008 model car is in an accident, and the headlights are broken. The insurance company will pay for the value of used 2008 headlights, not brand new 2024 headlights. If they paid for new parts, Ali would be better off than he was before the loss, which would violate the principle of indemnity.

  • Methods of Providing Indemnity

    • The insurance policy gives the insurer the option to provide indemnity in one of four ways, usually choosing the most cost-effective method:

      1. Cash Payment: The most common method, where the insurer pays the insured directly.

      2. Repair: The insurer arranges for the damaged item to be repaired (e.g., a damaged car).

      3. Replacement: The insurer replaces a lost or destroyed item with a similar one (e.g., a stolen piece of jewelry).

      4. Reinstatement: This refers to rebuilding property, such as a building damaged by fire.

  • Modifications to Strict Indemnity

    • Agreed Value Policies: For items where value is difficult to assess (like fine art or antiques), the insurer and insured agree on the value at the start of the policy. This agreed amount is what will be paid in the event of a total loss, regardless of the market value on the day of the loss.

    • Reinstatement ("New for Old") Cover: Common in home and commercial building insurance, this condition modifies indemnity by agreeing to pay the full cost of replacement or rebuilding with new materials, without any deduction for depreciation.

    • First Loss Insurance: Used when a total loss is highly unlikely (e.g., theft of all heavy machinery from a large warehouse). The insured insures for a lower amount that represents the maximum probable loss from a single event.

Topic 4: The Principle of Subrogation

  • The Core Concept: Stepping into the Insured's Shoes

    • Subrogation is a direct consequence of the principle of indemnity.

    • Definition: Subrogation is the right of an insurance company, after it has paid a claim to its policyholder, to take over the policyholder's legal rights to recover the loss from a third party who was responsible for it.

    • Purpose: It prevents the insured from being paid twice (once by their insurer and again by the at-fault party) and ensures that the person who caused the loss is ultimately held financially responsible.

    • Example: Ali is waiting at a red light when his car is hit from behind by Basim. Ali has comprehensive insurance, so his insurer pays for the repairs to his car. Through subrogation, Ali's insurer now has the right to sue Basim (or Basim's insurer) to recover the money they paid to Ali.

  • Subrogation and Salvage

    • Subrogation rights also extend to salvage.

    • If an item is damaged beyond repair and the insurer pays a total loss claim (e.g., for a "written-off" car), the insurer is entitled to take possession of the wreckage (the salvage) and sell it for scrap. If the insured kept both the full cash payment and the salvage, they would profit from the loss.

Topic 5: The Principle of Contribution

  • The Core Concept: Sharing the Burden

    • Contribution is another principle that supports indemnity. It applies when a risk is insured with more than one insurance company.

    • Definition: Contribution is the right of an insurer who has paid a loss to call upon other insurers who are also liable for the same loss to contribute their rateable proportion of the payment.

    • Purpose: It prevents an insured with double insurance from claiming the full loss from each insurer and making a profit.

    • Example: A warehouse worth SAR 1,000,000 is accidentally insured with Company A for SAR 1,000,000 and also with Company B for SAR 1,000,000. A fire causes SAR 200,000 of damage. The insured can claim the loss from either company, but not both. If they claim from Company A, Company A will pay the SAR 200,000 and then use the principle of contribution to recover SAR 100,000 from Company B, so they each pay their share.

  • Conditions for Contribution to Apply

    • For contribution to apply, six conditions must be met:

      1. Two or more policies of indemnity must be in force.

      2. The policies must cover the same interest (i.e., be for the same insured person).

      3. The policies must cover the same subject matter (e.g., the same building).

      4. The policies must cover the same peril that caused the loss (e.g., both cover fire).

      5. The policies must have coverage for the same loss.

      6. All policies must be in force at the time of the loss.

Topic 6: The Principle of Proximate Cause

  • The Core Concept: Finding the Real Cause

    • When a loss occurs, it is often the result of a chain of events. The principle of proximate cause is used to determine which event in the chain was the legally significant cause of the loss, in order to decide if the policy covers it.

    • Definition: The proximate cause is the active, efficient cause that sets in motion a chain of events which brings about a result, without the intervention of any force from a new and independent source. It is the dominant or most powerful cause, which is not necessarily the first or the last event in time.

  • Classifying Perils

    • To apply the principle, we must identify the proximate cause and then see how the policy treats that cause. Perils are classified in one of three ways:

      1. Insured Peril: A peril that is specifically stated as covered in the policy (e.g., Fire). If this is the proximate cause, the loss is covered.

      2. Excluded Peril: A peril that is specifically stated as not covered (e.g., War, Earthquake). If this is the proximate cause, the loss is not covered.

      3. Uninsured (or Unnamed) Peril: A peril that is not mentioned in the policy at all. If this is the proximate cause, the loss is not covered.

  • Example of a Chain of Events:

    • During a storm (uninsured peril), a tree is blown over and crashes through the roof of a house, leaving a large hole.

    • Heavy rain (uninsured peril) then enters through the hole, causing significant water damage to the contents.

    • The proximate cause of the water damage is not the rain itself, but the storm that created the hole in the roof. Since storm is an insured peril under most home policies, the resulting water damage would be covered as a direct consequence. If, however, the proximate cause was an excluded peril (e.g., an earthquake broke the roof), then the resulting water damage would not be covered.

Topic 7: The Insurance Contract and Its Structure

  • Definition and Elements

    • An insurance contract is a legal agreement where one party (the insurer) agrees to pay the other party (the insured) for a specified loss, in exchange for a payment called a premium.

    • Like any valid contract, it must have five key elements:

      1. Consent: A genuine "meeting of the minds" through offer and acceptance.

      2. Parties with Capacity: The parties must be legally capable of entering into a contract (e.g., not minors or mentally incompetent).

      3. Legitimate Subject Matter: The item being insured must be legal and permissible.

      4. Legitimate Purpose: The reason for the contract must be lawful.

      5. Consideration: Each party must give something of value. The insured gives the premium, and the insurer gives the promise to pay.

  • Structure of the Policy Document

    • While the contract exists once there is agreement, it is evidenced by a written policy document. All policies, regardless of style, contain eight main sections:

      1. Heading: Insurer's name and address.

      2. Preamble: A preliminary statement confirming that the proposal form is the basis of the contract and that consideration has been provided.

      3. Operative Clause: The most important part. It sets out precisely what the policy covers and the circumstances under which the insurer will pay.

      4. Exclusions: Details what the policy does not cover.

      5. Conditions: The rules that govern the behavior of both parties (e.g., duty of care, claims notification procedures).

      6. Cooperative Profit-Sharing Clause: A clause specific to cooperative insurance in KSA, detailing how any surplus will be distributed to policyholders.

      7. Signature: Signed by a senior official of the insurance company.

      8. Schedule: This section contains all the unique information that personalizes the standard policy for the individual insured, such as their name, address, policy period, sum insured, and premium.

  • Warranties and Endorsements

    • Warranty: A very strict type of policy condition. A breach of a warranty entitles the insurer to repudiate the entire contract, regardless of whether the breach is related to the loss.

    • Endorsement: A separate document that is attached to the policy to record any changes made during the policy period, such as changing a vehicle or increasing the sum insured. It becomes an integral part of the contract.
