Chapter 5: Procedures and Policies of the Insurance Process


Topic 1: The Organizational Structure of an Insurance Company

  • Introduction to Corporate Structure

    • To provide insurance services effectively, manage vast sums of money, and comply with regulations, insurance companies require a well-defined organizational structure.

    • This structure ensures that there are clear lines of responsibility, from the highest level of strategic oversight down to the daily operational tasks.

    • While the exact structure can vary based on a company's size and specialization, a standard model exists across the industry.

  • The Board of Directors: The Ultimate Authority

    • At the very top of any insurance company is the Board of Directors (BoD).

    • Role: The Board is not involved in the day-to-day running of the company. Instead, its primary role is governance and strategic oversight. They set the company's overall direction and ensure it is being managed responsibly and in the best interests of all stakeholders (shareholders, policyholders, and employees).

    • Composition: The Board is made up of members elected by the shareholders. In a regulated market like Saudi Arabia, all board members must be approved by supervisory authorities like SAMA to ensure they meet high professional and ethical standards.

    • Key Functions and Responsibilities:

      • Strategy: Setting the company's overall strategy, main objectives, and risk management policies.

      • Oversight: Monitoring the performance of the company and its senior management to ensure they are meeting their goals.

      • Financial Integrity: Ensuring the accuracy of the company's financial and accounting systems. They approve the annual budget and major capital expenditures.

      • Governance: Establishing and approving the company's internal rules, including policies on conflicts of interest, internal controls, and professional codes of conduct.

      • Protecting Policyholders: The Board has an ultimate responsibility to ensure that the interests of the insured are protected at all times.

  • Board of Directors Committees: Specialized Oversight

    • To handle its wide range of responsibilities effectively, the Board of Directors delegates specific tasks to smaller, specialized committees.

    • A. Executive Committee: Acts as a steering committee for the Board. It reviews the company's strategy and financial results in more detail and makes recommendations to the full Board.

    • B. Investment Committee:

      • Role: This committee is responsible for overseeing the company's investment activities. Insurance companies invest the premiums they collect, and this is a major source of their income.

      • Functions: They set the company's investment objectives, formulate the investment policy, and ensure all investments comply with regulatory restrictions.

    • C. Audit Committee:

      • Role: Think of the Audit Committee as the company's internal watchdog. Its job is to ensure financial integrity and compliance with laws.

      • Functions: They review the company's financial statements, oversee the work of both the internal and external auditors, and ensure that the company has adequate internal controls to prevent errors and fraud.

    • D. Nominations and Remunerations Committee:

      • Role: This committee deals with the people at the top.

      • Functions: They are responsible for recommending new members for the Board of Directors. They also establish the policies for the compensation (remuneration) of board members and senior executives, often linking pay to performance.

  • Key Senior Positions in an Insurance Company

    • These are the key professionals responsible for executing the company's strategy and managing its core functions.

    • A. Compliance Officer:

      • Role: This is a critical role in a highly regulated industry. The Compliance Officer is the company's rule-keeper.

      • Responsibility: They are responsible for ensuring that the company complies with all laws and regulations issued by SAMA, the CMA, and other regulatory bodies. They report directly to the Audit Committee, which gives them independence from the day-to-day management.

    • B. Internal Auditor:

      • Role: The Internal Auditor provides an independent assessment of the company's operations and internal controls.

      • Responsibility: They review the company's procedures to ensure they are efficient and effective, and they report their findings and recommendations for improvement to the Audit Committee.

    • C. Actuary:

      • Role: The actuary is the mathematical and statistical expert of the insurance company.

      • Responsibility: They are responsible for the financial heart of the company. Their key tasks include:

        • Pricing: Calculating the premiums for insurance products.

        • Reserving: Determining the amount of money the company must set aside (technical provisions) to pay future claims.

        • Solvency: Assessing the company's overall financial health and its ability to meet all its future obligations.

    • D. Senior Management (CEO / General Manager):

      • Role: Led by the Chief Executive Officer or General Manager, this team is responsible for the day-to-day management of the company.

      • Responsibility: They implement the strategic plans set by the Board, manage the company's daily activities, and report back to the Board on performance.

Topic 2: The Core Procedures of the Insurance Process

  • Once an insurance company is licensed and has its structure in place, it begins its daily work of providing insurance services. This process can be broken down into several key functions.

  • 5.2.1 Marketing and Sales: Reaching the Customer

    • This function is about making potential customers aware of the company's products and then completing the sale.

    • Marketing Channels: These are the methods used to promote the products.

      • Examples: TV and radio ads, newspaper ads, websites, social media, participating in conferences, and distributing brochures.

    • Sales Channels: These are the specific avenues through which a customer can buy a policy.

      • Direct Sale: The customer buys directly from the insurance company, either by visiting a branch or through its sales representatives.

      • Call Centers: Selling policies over the phone.

      • Online Sales: Customers purchase policies directly through the company's website or app. This is increasingly common for simple products like motor and travel insurance.

      • Insurance Intermediaries:

        • Insurance Agent: Represents one insurance company. Their job is to sell the products of that specific company.

        • Insurance Broker: Represents the customer. Their job is to survey the market and find the best policy from multiple different insurance companies to suit the client's needs.

      • Bancassurance: A partnership where insurance products are sold through the branches of a bank. This gives the insurance company access to the bank's large customer base.

  • 5.2.2 Underwriting: The Art of Risk Selection

    • The Core Concept: Underwriting is arguably the most critical function of an insurer. It is the process of evaluating, selecting, and pricing risks.

    • The Underwriter's Role: The underwriter is the professional who decides whether to accept or reject an insurance application. They are the company's primary risk assessor.

    • The Underwriting Process:

      1. Gather Information: The process starts with the information provided by the applicant on the proposal form. For complex risks, this may be supplemented by survey reports, medical reports, or financial statements.

      2. Assess the Risk: The underwriter analyzes the information to determine the level of risk. They look for hazards that might increase the likelihood or severity of a loss.

      3. Make a Decision: Based on the assessment, the underwriter makes one of three decisions:

        • Accept the risk: The application is approved as is.

        • Reject the risk: The application is declined because the risk is too high.

        • Accept with modifications: The risk is accepted, but with special terms, such as a higher premium, a higher deductible, or specific exclusions.

      4. Determine the Premium: Working with actuarial tables, the underwriter determines the final premium to be charged.

    • Importance: Good underwriting is essential for profitability. If an underwriter is too strict and charges premiums that are too high, the company will lose customers. If they are too lenient and charge premiums that are too low, the company will not collect enough money to pay its claims and will suffer a loss.

  • 5.2.3 Reinsurance: Managing the Insurer's Risk

    • The Core Concept: As we've learned, reinsurance is "insurance for insurance companies." It is the primary tool that an insurer uses to manage its own risk portfolio.

    • The Reinsurance Department's Role:

      • This department is responsible for managing the company's reinsurance contracts (treaties).

      • They decide how much of each risk the company will keep for itself (its retention) and how much it will transfer to a reinsurer.

      • When a very large claim occurs, this department is responsible for notifying the reinsurers and recovering the reinsured portion of the loss.

  • 5.2.4 Claims Handling: The Moment of Truth

    • The Core Concept: From the customer's perspective, the claims department is the most important part of the company. The way a company handles claims is the ultimate test of the promise it sold.

    • The Claims Process:

      1. Notification: The policyholder informs the insurer that a loss has occurred.

      2. Investigation: A claims adjuster investigates the claim. Their job is to determine the cause of the loss, confirm that the loss is covered by the policy, and assess the value of the damage. For large or complex claims, an independent loss adjuster may be appointed.

      3. Settlement: If the claim is valid, the company settles it by one of the methods of indemnity (cash, repair, replacement, or reinstatement).

    • Importance: A company's reputation is built on its ability to settle claims quickly and fairly. Poor claims service can quickly lead to a loss of customers and regulatory scrutiny.

  • 5.2.5 Finance and Investment

    • Finance/Accounting: This department manages all the money flowing in and out of the company. Its functions include preparing financial reports, managing cash flow, paying expenses and claims, and ensuring adequate reserves are maintained.

    • Investment: Insurance companies make profits from two sources: underwriting profit (premiums collected minus claims and expenses paid) and investment profit. The investment department invests the premiums received until they are needed to pay claims. Successful investment income allows the company to be more competitive with its pricing.

  • 5.2.6 Personnel and Risk Management

    • Personnel (HR): In a service industry like insurance, employees are the company's real capital. The HR department is responsible for hiring, training, and managing the company's staff.

    • Risk Management: This department is focused on the risks facing the insurance company itself. Its job is to identify, measure, and manage risks like operational failures, financial market volatility, and strategic threats, in order to protect the company's stability and success.
