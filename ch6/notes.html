Chapter 6: Risks and Obstacles of Insurance Companies

Introduction

  • Importance of Insurance: A robust insurance market is critical for a successful economy, supporting industries and protecting individuals and institutions. In Saudi Arabia, insurance contributes 1.48% to GDP.
  • Purpose: This chapter covers various risks and obstacles insurance companies face, with the aim that these challenges can be mitigated over time.
  • Learning Objective: Understand the different risks impacting insurance companies and how they are managed.

6.1 Product Development Risk

  • Definition: The risk associated with modifying existing products to meet customer needs or enhance marketability in a competitive environment. Changes can affect product coverage and liabilities, leading to potential risks.
  • Key Actions to Manage:
    • Conduct an actuarial review and obtain approval before launching new products.
    • Ensure compliance with Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) regulations for new product approvals.
    • Prepare a report tracking changes in risks and insured behavior post-launch.
  • Key Note: The risk lies in launching untested new products, not in the development process itself, which aims to improve existing products.
  • Example: An insurance company introduces a new health insurance plan but fails to test it adequately, leading to unexpected claims that exceed projections.

6.1.1 Underwriting Risk

  • Definition: The risk tied to the process of assessing risks presented to insurance companies for approval.
  • Key Actions to Manage:
    • Clearly articulate policy terms to avoid misinterpretation.
    • Ensure the insured completes the insurance application accurately.
    • Include all costs (e.g., marketing, indirect costs) in premium calculations.
    • Set clear underwriting guidelines to define departmental responsibilities (e.g., Sales, Claims, Reinsurance).
    • Reinsure part of the risk per SAMA’s Regulation of Reinsurance Activities to reduce exposure.
    • Conduct periodic reviews of policies, guidelines, and processes to ensure effectiveness.
  • Example: An unclear policy term leads to disputes over coverage, increasing claim settlement costs.

6.1.2 Pricing Risk

  • Definition: A risk arising from determining the appropriate premium rate, a function of underwriting.
  • Key Actions to Manage:
    • Consider all potential risks when setting prices.
    • Evaluate profits and losses to assess the impact of premium adjustments.
    • Initiate a re-pricing process if new risk patterns emerge.
    • Engage actuarial experts for accurate pricing.
  • Example: Underpricing a motor insurance policy due to inaccurate risk assessment leads to financial losses when claims exceed premiums.

6.1.3 Claims Settlement Risk

  • Definition: Risks associated with the claims payment process, where actual claims may exceed the expected insurance liabilities, particularly in competitive markets with frequent motor and medical claims.
  • Impact: Claims costs have caused losses exceeding 50% of some companies’ capital.
  • Key Actions to Manage:
    • Review claims decisions to ensure alignment with policy coverage.
    • Conduct periodic evaluations of claims settlement procedures to improve effectiveness.
    • Coordinate with reinsurers to streamline claims settlements.
  • Example: A company pays claims not covered by the policy due to poor review processes, leading to financial strain.

6.1.4 Solvency Risk

  • Definition: The risk that an insurance company lacks the financial ability to meet obligations to policyholders in a timely manner.
  • Importance: Solvency protects policyholders and ensures the company’s operational continuity.
  • Components: Solvency margin typically includes capital, reserves, and retained earnings, varying by country regulations.
  • Challenges:
    • Companies failing to meet obligations.
    • Ceasing to offer certain insurance types, reducing revenue.
    • Complete cessation of insurance offerings, further impacting resources.
  • SAMA’s Role: Implements regulations to ensure solvency through operational controls.
  • Example: A company unable to pay claims due to insufficient reserves faces regulatory penalties and loss of customer trust.

6.1.5 Credit Risk

  • Definition: The risk that a party (e.g., insured) fails to meet payment obligations, influenced by payment history and economic conditions.
  • Key Actions to Manage:
    • Require collateral or guarantees from the insured.
    • Implement strict payment schedules for premiums.
    • Restrict credit granting in terms of quality and quantity.
    • Periodically review credit policies to identify and address weaknesses.
  • Example: An insured defaults on premium payments, impacting the company’s cash flow.

6.1.6 Information Technology Risk

  • Definition: Risks arising from failures or errors in IT systems used for daily operations, such as policy management and data storage.
  • Key Actions to Manage:
    • Use secure and reliable IT systems.
    • Regularly update systems and develop a disaster recovery plan.
    • Install modern antivirus software on all devices.
    • Store financial and other data securely with backup copies.
    • Employ trained IT personnel.
  • Example: A system failure leads to loss of policyholder data, disrupting operations and risking data breaches.

6.2 Defrauding Insurance Companies

  • Learning Objective: Understand insurance fraud, its types, sources, and prevention methods.
  • Definition: Any act or negligence to obtain illegal gains, such as fake claims or misrepresentation.
  • Types of Fraud:
    • Primary Fraud: Claiming nonexistent accidents, injuries, or services.
    • Secondary Fraud: Exaggerating legitimate claims to increase payouts.
  • Sources of Fraud:
    • Internal Fraud: By employees, managers, or board members.
    • Policyholder Fraud: By insured individuals submitting false claims.
    • Broker/Agent Fraud: By intermediaries manipulating claims or policies.
    • Service Provider Fraud: By entities like medical centers or repair shops inflating bills.
  • Prevention Strategies:
    • Draft clear policies to minimize fraud opportunities.
    • Define customer acceptance criteria and claims evaluation procedures.
    • Inform policyholders of anti-fraud policies and consequences.
    • Balance fraud prevention with customer satisfaction goals.
  • Example: A policyholder submits a fake car accident claim to receive an undeserved payout.

6.2.2 Internal Fraud

  • Definition: Fraud committed by insiders (e.g., employees, managers) conspiring internally or externally.
  • Factors Increasing Risk:
    • Complex organizational structures.
    • Rapid technological advancements creating fraud opportunities.
    • Incentive structures tied to performance goals.
    • Economic instability (e.g., mergers, acquisitions).
    • Weak internal controls or centralized management.
  • Indicators:
    • Employees stealing company resources (e.g., equipment, data).
    • Bribery schemes involving third parties.
    • Undisclosed commissions from suppliers.
  • Example: An employee colludes with a supplier to award contracts in exchange for bribes, undetected due to weak controls.

6.2.3 Fraud Cost in the Insurance Sector

  • Nature: Fraud is a “hidden crime,” difficult to quantify due to undetected cases.
  • Impact:
    • Financial losses from paying fraudulent claims.
    • Increased premiums to offset losses, leading to customer loss.
    • Cessation of certain insurance products, disrupting operations.
    • Reduced investment capacity due to claim payouts.
    • Costs of investigations, legal actions, and hiring experts.
    • Damage to employee morale and company reputation.
  • Example: A company raises premiums to cover fraud losses, causing competitive disadvantage and customer churn.

6.3 SAMA’s Role in Fighting Insurance Fraud

  • Role: SAMA enforces regulations to combat fraud, requiring companies to implement robust internal controls and comply with anti-fraud measures.
  • Resources: SAMA’s website (http://www.sama.gov.sa/ar-sa/Laws/Pages/Insurance.aspx) provides detailed rules and regulations.
  • Example: SAMA mandates annual fraud prevention plans to enhance transparency and compliance.

6.4 Obstacles Facing Insurance Companies in KSA

6.4.1 Lack of Qualified Human Resources

  • Issue: Shortage of skilled professionals in the insurance sector due to:
    • Novelty of the industry in KSA (post-2004 regulations).
    • Limited specialized educational and training institutions.
    • Competition for talent and high turnover.
    • Social perceptions deterring careers in insurance.
  • SAMA’s Response: Requires companies to include training plans in annual strategies.
  • Example: A company struggles to hire actuaries, impacting product pricing accuracy.

6.4.2 Insurance Awareness and Education

  • Issue: Low penetration rate in KSA due to:
    • Recent development of the insurance industry.
    • Limited media coverage of insurance benefits.
    • Negative social perceptions and lack of educational programs.
    • Non-mandatory insurance products (e.g., homeowners’ insurance).
  • Impact: Slow market growth and reluctance to pursue insurance careers.
  • Example: Lack of awareness leads to low uptake of comprehensive motor insurance.

6.5 Reinsurance Risk

  • Definition: Risks from transferring part of the insurance risk to reinsurers, who may fail to meet obligations.
  • Impact: Companies remain liable to policyholders despite reinsurance agreements.
  • Example: A reinsurer’s refusal to cover flood-related claims increases the insurer’s financial burden.

6.6 Reputation Risk

  • Definition: Risks from negative public or competitor reviews, affecting customer relationships and revenue.
  • Causes: Delays in claims processing or poor service quality.
  • Example: Negative online reviews about slow claim approvals deter new customers.

6.7 Non-Compliance Risk

  • Definition: Risks from violating SAMA’s regulations or internal policies.
  • Key Actions:
    • Ensure compliance with all regulatory requirements.
    • Monitor instructions and payment policies.
    • Maintain robust contractual relationships.
  • Example: Selling unapproved products leads to regulatory fines.

6.8 Changes in Country Risk

  • Definition: Risks from macroeconomic or political changes affecting profitability (e.g., inflation, instability).
  • Example: A recession increases claim frequencies, straining company finances.

6.9 Money Laundering and Terrorism Financing Risk

  • Definition:
    • Money Laundering: Concealing the origin of illegally obtained funds to appear legitimate.
    • Terrorism Financing: Funding terrorist activities, even with legitimate funds.
  • Stages of Money Laundering:
    1. Placement: Introducing illegal funds into financial systems (e.g., cash deposits, policy purchases).
    2. Layering: Complex transactions to obscure fund origins (e.g., wire transfers, fake investments).
    3. Integration: Reintroducing funds as legitimate through assets or investments.
  • Indicators:
    • Delayed or incomplete customer information.
    • Large lump-sum payments for policies.
    • Policy cancellations with refunds to unrelated third parties.
  • SAMA’s Role: Mandates AML/CFT compliance, including internal controls and reporting to the Financial Investigation Unit.
  • Example: An insured uses a policy to launder money by paying premiums with illicit funds and requesting early refunds.

Revision Questions

  1. Product Development Risk: Risk from modifying products without adequate testing, affecting coverage and liabilities. (Ref: 6-1)
  2. Claims Settlement Risk Management: Review decisions, evaluate procedures, coordinate with reinsurers. (Ref: 6-1-3)
  3. Solvency Risk: Inability to meet financial obligations to policyholders, impacting continuity. (Ref: 6-4-1)
  4. Credit Risk Management: Require collateral, enforce payment schedules, limit credit, review policies. (Ref: 6-1-5)
  5. IT Risk Management: Use secure systems, update regularly, install antivirus, maintain backups, train staff. (Ref: 6-1-6)
  6. Money Laundering Stages: Placement, Layering, Integration. (Ref: 6-9)
  7. Internal Fraud Indicators: Resource theft, bribery, undisclosed commissions, weak controls. (Ref: 6-2-2)
  8. Pricing Risk: Risk from inaccurate premium setting, requiring actuarial expertise and risk assessment. (Ref: 6-1-2)
  9. Fraud Methods and Effects: Fake claims, fraudulent methods, unqualified claims, false identities; lead to financial losses, higher premiums, reduced investments, and reputational damage. (Ref: 6-1)

Key Takeaways

  • Insurance companies face diverse risks (product, underwriting, pricing, claims, solvency, credit, IT, fraud, etc.).

  • Effective management requires regulatory compliance, robust internal controls, and proactive measures.

  • SAMA plays a critical role in enforcing regulations to ensure solvency and combat fraud.

  • Challenges like low awareness and human resource shortages hinder the KSA insurance market’s growth.

  • The Regulatory Framework in Saudi Arabia

    • SAMA has implemented a comprehensive anti-financial crime framework that all insurance companies must follow.

    • The rules are designed to prevent, detect, and deter money laundering and terrorism financing activities.

  • Key Responsibilities of Insurance Companies

    • Appointing a Compliance Officer: Every company must appoint a qualified Compliance Officer who is responsible for overseeing the company's anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorism financing (CTF) program.

    • Customer Due Diligence (CDD) / "Know Your Customer" (KYC):

      • This is a fundamental requirement. Companies must verify the identity of every customer before entering into a business relationship.

      • This involves obtaining and verifying official identification documents (like the National ID or Iqama) and understanding the nature of the customer's business and the source of their funds.

    • Employee Training: All relevant employees, especially those in sales, underwriting, and claims, must receive regular training on how to identify and deal with suspicious activity.

    • Record Keeping: Companies must keep detailed records of all customer transactions for a minimum period (typically 10 years) to assist with any future investigations.

    • Reporting Suspicious Transactions:

      • If an employee identifies a transaction that they suspect may be related to money laundering or terrorism financing, they must report it internally to the Compliance Officer.

      • The Compliance Officer then investigates, and if the suspicion remains, they must file a

        Suspicious Transaction Report (STR) with the relevant government authority, the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU).

      • This reporting is a legal obligation and is crucial for enabling law enforcement to track and prosecute financial criminals.
