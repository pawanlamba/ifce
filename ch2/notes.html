Chapter 2: The Nature of the Insurance Sector in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia


Topic 1: The Rationale for Regulating the Insurance Industry

  • Why Governments Intervene

    • Even in the most free-market economies, governments find it necessary to monitor and regulate their insurance industries. This is not about controlling the market but about protecting the public and ensuring stability.

    • Insurance companies are unique because they handle enormous sums of public money. They collect premiums today in exchange for a promise to pay for future, uncertain losses.

    • This makes them fiduciaries—they are entrusted with funds that belong to their policyholders and shareholders. Regulation ensures this trust is not misplaced.

  • The Core Objectives of Insurance Regulation

    • Across the globe, the primary goals of insurance regulation are consistent and can be broken down into three main objectives:

      1. Maintaining Insurer Solvency: This is the most critical objective. "Solvency" means the insurer's ability to pay its debts and meet its long-term obligations. Regulators must ensure that an insurance company remains financially healthy enough to pay all legitimate claims, even in the event of a major disaster.

        • Example: If a major storm hits a city, a home insurer will receive thousands of claims at once. Solvency regulation ensures the insurer has enough capital reserves to pay all those claims without going bankrupt.

      2. Ensuring Fair Treatment of Consumers: Regulation aims to protect policyholders and beneficiaries from unfair or deceptive practices. This includes ensuring that insurance contracts are clear, marketing is not misleading, claims are handled promptly and fairly, and brokers act in the best interests of their clients.

      3. Making Certain Insurance Compulsory: Governments often mandate certain types of insurance to achieve broad societal protection. This ensures that victims of accidents receive compensation and that essential services are accessible.

        • Example: Compulsory motor third-party liability insurance ensures that if a driver injures someone or damages their property, there is a source of funds to cover the victim's medical bills and repair costs, regardless of the at-fault driver's personal financial situation.

Topic 2: Historical Background of the Insurance Sector in KSA

  • The Pre-Regulation Era

    • Before 2003, the insurance market in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia was largely unregulated. There were over 75 insurance companies operating in the market without a formal, overarching government supervision framework.

  • The Establishment of a National Company (Tawuniya)

    • In 1985 (1405 AH), a significant step was taken with the establishment of the National Cooperative Insurance Company (now known as Tawuniya) by Royal Decree.

    • It was created as a joint-stock company with its founding shareholders being major public funds (the Public Investment Fund, the Pension Fund, and the General Organization for Social Insurance).

    • The primary motivation was to create a Saudi insurance company that operated on the principle of cooperative insurance as an alternative to conventional commercial insurance.

    • Tawuniya's structure was unique: it was required to manage two separate accounts: one for its policyholders (reflecting the mutual, cooperative model) and one for its stockholders (reflecting the commercial model).

  • The Shift to a Regulated Market

    • The turning point for the entire industry was the issuance of the Cooperative Insurance Companies Control Law by Royal Decree in August 2003.

    • This legislation marked the beginning of formal government supervision of the insurance sector, transforming the Saudi market into one of the most regulated and structured in the region.

Topic 3: Key Drivers Behind Insurance Regulation in KSA

  • Two major factors created the urgent need for a comprehensive regulatory framework for insurance in the Kingdom.

  • A. Mandatory Medical Insurance

    • For many years, healthcare in Saudi Arabia was provided free of charge to both Saudi citizens and the large population of foreign residents.

    • With millions of foreign workers and their dependents residing in the Kingdom, this placed a significant financial burden on the state's healthcare system and the overall economy.

    • To address this, the government issued the Cooperative Health Insurance Law in 1999. This law made it compulsory for employers to provide private health insurance for all their non-Saudi employees and their families.

    • The law explicitly states that a residence permit (Iqama) cannot be issued or renewed without a valid health insurance policy that covers the entire duration of the permit.

    • This single piece of legislation created a massive, immediate demand for health insurance that was far too large for one company (Tawuniya) to handle. It also aligned with the government's broader policy of encouraging private sector participation in the economy.

  • B. Accession to the World Trade Organization (WTO)

    • The second major driver was the Kingdom's goal of joining the World Trade Organization.

    • A key requirement of the WTO accession agreement was the opening of the Saudi financial services market, including the insurance sector, to foreign investment.

    • This necessitated the creation of a clear, fair, and transparent legal framework that would regulate the market and create a level playing field for both local and foreign insurance companies.

    • The Cooperative Insurance Companies Control Law (2003) and its subsequent Implementing Regulations (2004) were the direct result, officially launching the modern, regulated insurance industry in the Kingdom.

Topic 4: The Main Regulatory and Supervisory Bodies

  • The insurance industry in KSA does not operate in a vacuum. It is an interactive and participatory industry that intersects with many different government and private agencies. Understanding these key players is essential to understanding the market.

  • 4.1 The Saudi Central Bank (SAMA)

    • Primary Role: SAMA is the main regulator and supervisor of the insurance sector in the Kingdom. It is a highly respected institution, also responsible for regulating the banking sector since 1952.

    • Insurance Supervision Department: Within SAMA, a specific department is responsible for the day-to-day supervision and control of the insurance industry.

    • Key Functions and Powers related to Insurance:

      • Licensing: SAMA is responsible for licensing all insurance companies, reinsurance companies, and insurance-related professional firms (like brokers and agents) that wish to operate in the Kingdom.

      • Regulation: It prepares and enforces the Implementing Regulations of the Insurance Law, setting the rules for the entire industry.

      • Financial Solvency: It sets the minimum capital requirements and solvency margins that companies must maintain to ensure they can pay claims.

      • Market Conduct: It regulates how companies deal with customers, sell products, and disclose information to ensure fairness and transparency.

      • Product Approval: Insurance companies must get their insurance products and policies approved by SAMA before they can be sold to the public.

      • Investment Control: It regulates the investments of insurance companies to prevent them from taking on excessive risk with policyholder funds.

      • Saudization: It promotes the development of the sector by setting requirements for training and job localization (hiring and qualifying Saudi nationals).

  • 4.2 Council of Cooperative Health Insurance (CCHI)

    • Primary Role: The CCHI is an independent government body established specifically to oversee the implementation of the mandatory Cooperative Health Insurance Law. Its board is chaired by the Minister of Health.

    • Key Functions:

      • Accrediting Healthcare Providers: It accredits all hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, and other medical facilities that want to provide services under the health insurance scheme. This ensures they meet quality standards.

      • Qualifying Insurance Companies: An insurance company must first get its license from SAMA, and then it must be qualified by the CCHI to be able to sell cooperative health insurance policies.

      • Protecting Policyholder Rights: It sets the rules that insurance companies must follow, such as the minimum benefits that must be included in the unified health policy, time limits for approving treatments (e.g., within 60 minutes), and time limits for paying claims to providers (e.g., within 60 days).

  • 4.3 Capital Market Authority (CMA)

    • Primary Role: The Cooperative Insurance Companies Control Law requires that all insurance companies in KSA must be public joint-stock companies. This means they must list a portion of their shares on the stock market (Tadawul) for public trading.

    • Connection to Insurance: Because all insurers are publicly listed, they fall under the supervision of the CMA.

    • Key Functions:

      • Investor Protection: The CMA's main goal is to protect investors by ensuring fairness, efficiency, and transparency in the market.

      • Disclosure: It sets strict rules for what information companies must disclose to the public and when. This includes publishing quarterly and annual financial statements within specific deadlines.

      • Preventing Market Manipulation: It investigates and acts against any illegal practices like fraud, insider trading, or providing unlicensed investment advice.

  • 4.4 Other Key Governmental Bodies

    • Ministry of Commerce (MC): Responsible for company registration. Every insurance company must have a Commercial Register (CR) issued by the MC, which is a prerequisite for getting a license from SAMA. They also ensure compliance with the national Companies Law.

    • Ministry of Investment (MISA): The gateway for foreign investment. Any insurance company with non-Saudi investors is subject to MISA's terms. MISA works to attract foreign capital and facilitate the process for international businesses.

    • Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (HRSD): Enforces the Labor Law. This includes ensuring that all private sector employers comply with the regulation to provide health insurance for their Saudi and non-Saudi employees.

    • Chambers of Commerce and Industry: Non-governmental bodies that represent and support the private sector. Every licensed insurance company must be a member of the local Chamber of Commerce.

Topic 5: Licensed Companies and Professionals in the KSA Market

  • The Saudi insurance market is composed of several types of licensed entities, from large insurance companies to specialized individual practitioners. All are licensed and supervised by SAMA.

  • 5.1 Insurance and Reinsurance Companies

    • Key Characteristics:

      • All are licensed by SAMA.

      • All must be public joint-stock companies, listed on the stock market and supervised by the CMA.

      • All must operate under the cooperative insurance model.

      • All must meet a minimum capital requirement of SAR 100 million.

    • Licensing Process: To get a license, a company must submit a detailed application to SAMA, which includes its Articles of Association, a comprehensive five-year business plan, a feasibility study, and an irrevocable bank guarantee for its capital.

  • 5.2 Insurance Profession Companies and Practitioners

    • These are the intermediaries and experts who provide essential services within the insurance ecosystem. SAMA licenses both companies and self-employed individuals in these professions.

    • General Requirements: To be licensed, practitioners typically need a university degree, at least five years of relevant experience or a specialized insurance certification, and must pass an approved exam. They also need to have professional liability insurance.

  • The Six Licensed Insurance Professions:

    1. Insurance Broker:

      • Role: A broker is a legal person who represents the client (the policyholder). They negotiate with multiple insurance companies to find the best coverage and price for their client. They work for the buyer of insurance.

      • Capital: Minimum capital for a brokerage company is SAR 3 million.

    2. Insurance Agent:

      • Role: An agent is a legal person who represents the insurance company. They act on behalf of one specific insurer to market and sell that company's policies. They work for the seller of insurance.

      • Capital: Minimum capital for an agency is SAR 500,000.

    3. Actuary:

      • Role: An actuary is a highly specialized professional who applies probability and statistical theory to the business of insurance. They are responsible for pricing products (calculating premiums), establishing financial reserves, and ensuring the company's long-term solvency. Every insurance company is required to have an actuary.

      • Capital: Minimum capital for an actuarial services company is SAR 150,000.

    4. Surveyor and Loss Adjuster:

      • Role: This professional has two main functions:

        • Surveyor (Pre-Loss): They inspect and assess an asset (like a factory or a ship) before it is insured to identify risks and help the underwriter price the policy correctly.

        • Loss Adjuster (Post-Loss): After a loss occurs, they investigate the cause of the damage, estimate the value of the loss, and help determine the insurer's liability under the policy.

      • Capital: Minimum capital is SAR 500,000.

    5. Insurance Claims Adjuster (Third-Party Administrator - TPA):

      • Role: A TPA is a company that manages, reviews, and settles insurance claims on behalf of an insurance company. They are very common in the health insurance sector, where they handle the administrative burden of processing millions of medical claims.

      • Example: GlobeMed and MedNet are well-known TPAs in the region.

      • Capital: Minimum capital is SAR 3 million.

    6. Insurance Consultant:

      • Role: A consultant provides expert advice on insurance matters. Unlike a broker, their fee is collected directly from the client to whom the advice is provided, and they may not be involved in the actual transaction of selling a policy.

      • Capital: Minimum capital is SAR 150,000.
