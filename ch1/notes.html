IFCE Exam Prep

Chapter 1: Risk and Insurance Management Foundations


Topic 1: The Foundational Idea of Insurance

  • The Core Concept: Cooperation and Shared Burdens

    • At its heart, the idea of insurance is built on the principle of cooperation among members of a community or society.

    • The fundamental goal is to collectively bear a loss that might affect one individual, distributing the financial impact across all members of the group.

    • This means that insurance isn't about preventing bad things from happening, but about sharing the cost when they do. The mechanism is designed to reduce the monetary effect of a loss, not to stop the risk itself.

    • In its most basic form, this cooperative sharing of risk has been practiced informally since the earliest human communities.

  • Ancient Roots of Insurance Principles

    • While the concept is ancient, the first written references to practices resembling modern insurance date back thousands of years.

    • The Code of Hammurabi (Babylonian Era): This ancient legal code contained provisions for types of insurance we would recognize today, such as theft and marine insurance. This demonstrates that societies have long sought ways to manage the financial consequences of unforeseen events.

    • The Law of Rhodes (Roman Empire): This law introduced a concept known as "General Average," a cornerstone of marine insurance that is still relevant today.

      • Example: Imagine a cargo ship caught in a severe storm. To prevent the ship from sinking, the captain orders a portion of the cargo to be thrown overboard (jettisoned) to lighten the load.

      • The Law of General Average dictates that the financial loss of that sacrificed cargo should not be borne solely by the cargo's owner. Instead, the loss is shared proportionally by all parties who had a financial stake in the voyage's success.

      • This includes the owner of the ship, the owners of all the other cargo on board, and even the crew (in the form of their wages). This is a perfect historical example of distributing a single loss among a large group to make it manageable.

  • Early Cooperative Practices in the Arabian Peninsula

    • Long before the formal establishment of insurance companies, historical examples of cooperative risk sharing can be found in the Arabian Peninsula.

    • Families, tribes, and relatives would voluntarily contribute financial resources into a common fund.

    • This fund was used as a means to help members of the community who were in need, providing a safety net based on mutual support and cooperation.

    • These practices were recognized and affirmed by the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) and later evolved into more formal institutions within the Islamic state around 650 AD. This illustrates a deep-rooted cultural history of mutual aid that aligns with the core principles of insurance.

Topic 2: The Evolution of Insurance from Cooperation to Commerce

  • The Cooperative Stage in Europe

    • Following the early historical examples, the concept of cooperative insurance saw significant development in Europe, particularly through the efforts of Workers Unions.

    • The most prominent example from this period was the emergence of fire insurance.

    • Mechanism: Members of the same trade or profession would pool their money into a common fund. This fund was often supplemented by charitable contributions.

    • The purpose of the fund was to provide financial compensation to any member whose property was damaged by fire or who suffered another type of accident.

    • This model reinforced the definition of insurance as a collaboration where a group of people collectively bears the damage that affects a small number of them.

  • The Rise of Commercial Insurance

    • The purely cooperative stage eventually gave way to the appearance of "middle-men" and the establishment of formal insurance and reinsurance companies as commercial enterprises.

    • This marked a significant shift from informal, voluntary aid to a structured, business-oriented model.

    • The modern form of insurance business evolved into public joint stock companies, which operate based on a sophisticated framework of different insurance contracts, statistical analysis, laws, and established principles.

  • The Modern Insurance Mechanism

    • The modern insurance system is managed by insurance companies, which are often supported by reinsurance companies. Reinsurance is essentially insurance for insurance companies, a topic we will explore in detail later.

    • The Process:

      1. Premium Collection: Insurance companies manage the process by collecting payments, known as premiums, from a large number of individuals or businesses (the insured).

      2. Risk Assessment: Premiums are not random. They are carefully determined for each policyholder based on the specific chances of a loss occurring and the potential size of that damage.

      3. Pooling of Funds: The collected premiums are placed into a large fund, often referred to as an "insurance pool".

      4. Claims Payment: When an insured person suffers a loss that is covered by their policy, the insurance company pays for the damages out of this pool. This is how the losses of the few are paid for by the contributions of the many.

  • The Insurance Contract (Policy)

    • The relationship between the individual (the insured) and the insurance company (the insurer) is formalized through a legal contract called an "insurance contract" or "insurance policy".

    • This contract turns the insurer into a guarantor who is responsible for the financial losses of the insured.

    • It explicitly details the obligations and the rights of both parties. It specifies what risks are covered, the limits of the coverage, the conditions that must be met, and the duties of each party.

    • This contract must be fulfilled by both sides in accordance with the terms laid out in the policy and any relevant laws governing the industry.

Topic 3: Understanding the Concept of Risk

  • Risk: The Foundation of the Insurance Industry

    • Risk is the single most important and substantial component of the insurance industry.

    • An effective analogy is to compare risk's role in insurance to the engine's role in the automotive industry; one simply cannot exist without the other.

    • A deep understanding of risk is therefore essential before one can understand insurance itself.

  • Defining Risk

    • The term "risk" can be elusive, and it has been defined in several ways by academics and practitioners. Common definitions include:

      • "Uncertainty of a loss occurring".

      • "The possibility of an outcome being different from the expected".

      • "The possibilities of having a negative outcome of any incidents".

    • The common thread in all these definitions is a

      lack of complete knowledge about the future. When we talk about risk, there is a state of doubt or suspicion about whether a future event will happen.

    • Crucially, the word "risk" implies not just uncertainty, but uncertainty about a

      negative or undesirable outcome. It suggests a future that may leave us in a financial or physical position that is worse than our current one.

  • The Core Elements of Risk

    • We can break down the concept of risk into four key elements that are present in any risky situation:

      1. Incidents or Situations: There must be some event or condition that could occur. For example, driving a car presents the situation of a potential collision.

      2. Possibility of Undesirable Occurrence: The outcome of the event must have the potential to be negative. A collision has the undesirable outcome of vehicle damage or injury.

      3. Inability to Predict the Future Impact: We cannot know with certainty what the exact future impact of the risk will be compared to our current situation. We don't know

        if an accident will happen, or how severe it will be.

      4. Uncertainty of Loss or No Loss: The outcome is not guaranteed. There is a chance of a loss, but also a chance of no loss at all. You might complete your journey safely with no incident.

  • Synthesizing the Elements

    • When these elements are combined, they create the overall feeling and reality of risk. The key takeaways are:

      • The Idea of Doubt: Risk creates a moral or psychological feeling of fear or anxiety about a potential loss from an unintentional incident.

      • Varying Levels: The definitions imply that there can be different degrees or levels of risk; some situations are riskier than others.

      • Multiple Causes: The negative results or outcomes can arise from a single cause or from multiple contributing causes.

Topic 4: Objective Risk vs. Subjective Risk

  • The Importance of Differentiating Risk Types

    • Because the general definition of risk involves uncertainty, it's crucial for insurance purposes to distinguish between two fundamental types of risk: Objective Risk and Subjective Risk. One is measurable and manageable for an insurer, while the other is not.

  • Objective Risk (The Degree of Risk)

    • Definition: Objective risk is defined as the relative difference between the actual loss and the expected loss. It is a statistical and measurable concept, which is why it is also called the "degree of risk" or "objectivity of risk".

    • Example: Car Insurance

      • Imagine an insurance company that covers 100,000 cars.

      • Using historical data collected over many years, the company's statisticians calculate that, on average, 1% of these cars suffer a total loss each year. This means they

        expect 1,000 cars to be lost. This figure (1,000 cars) is the

        Expected Loss.

      • However, in reality, it is highly unlikely that exactly 1,000 cars will be totaled in the upcoming year. The number could be higher (e.g., 1,100 cars) or lower (e.g., 900 cars). The number that actually ends up being lost is the

        Actual Loss.

      • Objective Risk is this variation or gap between the expected (1,000) and the actual (e.g., 1,100) loss.

    • Insurability: Insurance companies can work with and insure against objective risk. The entire business model of insurance is based on reducing the gap between expected and actual losses. They achieve this primarily by increasing the number of people in the insurance pool (the Law of Large Numbers), which makes predictions more accurate.

  • Subjective Risk (The Feeling of Risk)

    • Definition: Subjective risk is defined as the uncertainty that arises from an individual's personal judgment, intuition, or state of mind. It is a belief that something might happen without any supporting scientific evidence or logical reasoning.

    • Example: The Canceled Flight

      • A person is on their way to the airport for a flight.

      • While on the way, they see a person whom they dislike.

      • They interpret this random encounter as a "bad sign" or omen and develop a strong feeling that something terrible is going to happen to the airplane.

      • Based purely on this intuition and not on any logical reason (like a weather report or news about the airline), they decide to cancel their flight.

    • Insurability: Insurance companies cannot deal with or insure subjective risk. The reason is that it is not based on any measurable, scientific facts. It varies wildly from one person to another and depends entirely on their personal judgment, fears, and superstitions. There is no way for an insurer to calculate a premium for a feeling.

Topic 5: How the Term "Risk" is Used in the Insurance Industry

  • A Multi-Faceted Term

    • In everyday conversation, "risk" has a general meaning of danger or uncertainty. However, within the insurance industry, the word is used in several different and specific metaphorical ways. Understanding these different contexts is key to understanding how insurance professionals talk about their work. When someone in insurance says "risk," they could be referring to one of the following concepts.

  • 1. Risk as the Event or Incident

    • The term "risk" is often used to describe the specific event or incident that, if it occurs, obligates the insurance company to pay compensation for the resulting loss.

    • Example: A property insurance policy might cover the "risk of fire" in a building, or a car insurance policy might cover the "risk of car accidents". Here, "risk" is a synonym for the peril or the cause of the loss. Other examples would be the risk of theft, shipwreck, or earthquake.

  • 2. Risk as the Insured Property

    • Insurance professionals frequently refer to the object being insured as "the risk."

    • Example: An underwriter looking at a new application might ask their colleague, "What do you think of this risk?" while pointing to the details of a specific house or vehicle. In this context, "the risk" refers to the property itself—the house, the car, the shipment of goods, or the ship that is subject to potential loss.

  • 3. Risk as a Description of Quality or Nature

    • "Risk" can be used as an adjective to describe the quality or nature of the item being insured, specifically regarding how likely it is to suffer a loss.

    • Example: An underwriter might label an application as a "poor risk." This doesn't mean the house itself is bad, but that the probability of a loss occurring is high. This could be due to factors like its construction (e.g., all wood), its location (e.g., in a flood zone), or its use (e.g., storing flammable materials). Conversely, a "good risk" would have a low probability of loss.

  • 4. Risk as the Level of Probability

    • Similar to the above, "risk" can be used to directly describe the probability of a loss happening.

    • Example: If there is a very high probability that an event will occur, it will be described simply as a "high risk" situation. For instance, insuring a home on an unstable cliffside against landslide would be considered a very high risk.

  • The Best Definition for Insurance Purposes

    • While all the above uses are common, the most comprehensive and useful definition of risk from an insurance standpoint is:

    • "The possible financial loss to income or fortune because of certain incidents that harm individuals, properties or third parties".

    • This definition is powerful because it contains several key elements that are essential for insurability:

      • The loss must be

        financial, not purely emotional or moral.

      • The loss

        may or may not occur; it cannot be something that is certain to happen, nor can it be impossible.

      • The loss can happen to a

        person (e.g., injury), to their properties (e.g., a house fire), or to third parties for whom they are legally responsible (e.g., causing a car accident that injures someone else).

Topic 6: Risk Classification (Part 1): Financial vs. Non-Financial Risks

  • Why We Classify Risks

    • We have defined risk as a situation that could result in a loss to a person or property. However, not all losses are the same. To determine which risks can be handled by insurance, we must classify them. The first major classification is based on whether the outcome of the risk can be measured in money. This gives us two categories: Financial Risks and Non-Financial Risks.

  • Financial Risk

    • Definition: A financial risk is any situation where the resulting loss can be specified and measured in financial or monetary terms. The focus here is not on the risk event itself, but on the outcome; if the result of the incident can be valued in currency, it is a financial risk.

    • These are the types of risks that insurance companies are designed to handle. Financial risks are considered

      insurable and are readily accepted by insurers because a clear monetary value can be assigned to the loss.

    • Examples of Financial Risks:

      • Property Damage: A fire damages a building, a flood ruins inventory, or a hailstorm dents a car. The cost to repair the building, replace the inventory, or fix the car can be precisely calculated.

      • Theft: If goods are stolen from a warehouse, their value can be determined from invoices and market prices.

      • Business Interruption: If a fire shuts down a factory, the loss of income and profits during the closure period can be financially measured.

      • Liability: If you cause a car accident that injures another person, the court can identify and financially measure the damages you are responsible for, such as their medical bills and lost wages.

  • Non-Financial Risk

    • Definition: A non-financial risk is a situation where the outcome cannot be measured in financial terms. These risks are very difficult to identify and measure because their effects are primarily psychological, emotional, or sentimental, and these feelings vary greatly between people and circumstances.

    • The outcome of a non-financial risk is usually measured by personal characteristics like joy, pleasure, disappointment, or unhappiness, not in dollars, riyals, or euros.

    • Insurance companies do

      not accept or insure non-financial risks. They cannot do so because there is no accurate, objective metric to measure the size of the loss, which is a fundamental requirement for the insurance industry.

    • Examples of Non-Financial Risks:

      • Disappointing Purchase: You decide to buy a new car, but after a week you realize you feel uncomfortable driving it and regret the purchase. This feeling of dissatisfaction is a loss to you, but it has no objective financial value.

      • Choice of Entertainment: Deciding to visit a new restaurant for the first time involves the risk that the outcome will be disappointment rather than enjoyment.

      • Career Choice: Selecting a new job involves a degree of risk regarding unknown outcomes; you might end up loving it or hating it. While this choice has financial implications, the core outcome of job satisfaction is not something that can be precisely measured in money.

      • Sentimental Value: A person has a single photograph of themselves as a child with their father who has since passed away. To that person, the photograph has immense sentimental value and is irreplaceable. However, this value is purely emotional; we cannot assign a financial figure to it. To someone else, the same photograph might have no value at all.

Topic 7: Risk Classification (Part 2): Pure vs. Speculative Risks

  • A Crucial Distinction for Insurability

    • The second major way we classify risk is by looking at the possible outcomes of the event. This distinction between pure and speculative risk is critically important because it directly determines whether a risk is insurable by a private insurance company. The types of risk concerned here are financial, pure, or speculative, and this classification is about the consequences of events.

  • Pure Risk

    • Definition: A pure risk is a situation that has only two possible outcomes: a loss or no loss. There is no possibility of financial gain. The outcome is either undesirable (a loss occurs) or it leaves you in the exact same financial state you were in before the event (no loss occurred).

    • The "Loss or Break-Even" Scenario: You can only lose or break even; you cannot come out ahead.

    • Insurability: Pure risks are the types of risks that insurance companies accept to insure. The entire structure of traditional insurance is built around managing and compensating for pure risks.

    • Examples of Pure Risks:

      • Driving a Car: Every time you drive, you face the risk of an accident. If you have an accident, you suffer a financial loss from damage to the vehicle and potential injuries. If you do not have an accident, your financial position is unaltered; you simply arrive safely. There is no opportunity to make a profit from the act of driving.

      • Property Ownership: Owning a building exposes you to the pure risk of a fire. If a fire occurs, you lose. If no fire occurs, you break even. You cannot profit from the fire.

      • Natural Disasters: Events like earthquakes or shipwrecks are pure risks. They can cause immense loss, but if they do not happen, you are simply left in your original state.

      • Personal Health: The risk of falling ill is a pure risk. Sickness can lead to significant financial loss through medical bills and lost income, but a state of good health does not generate a profit.

  • Speculative Risk

    • Definition: A speculative risk is a situation that has three possible outcomes: a loss, no loss, or a profit. These are risks that are taken on voluntarily in the hope of a financial gain.

    • The "Loss, Break-Even, or Gain" Scenario: You can lose money, break even, or make a profit.

    • Insurability: Speculative risks are not insurable by traditional insurance companies. The potential for the insured to make a profit from the risk creates a moral hazard that is fundamentally incompatible with the insurance principle of indemnification (making someone whole again, not better off). Society might benefit from innovation and speculation, but it would be against public policy to encourage gambling by insuring against losses while allowing the speculator to keep the gains.

    • Examples of Speculative Risks:

      • Investing in Stocks: If you buy 1,000 shares in a company, you are taking a speculative risk. If the share price falls, you will suffer a financial loss. If the price goes up, you will make a profit. If the price stays the same, you break even.

      • Starting a Business: Launching a new product is a classic speculative risk. Consumers may love the product, leading to large profits for the company. Conversely, they may dislike it, leading to a significant loss.

      • Gambling: Betting on a horse race is a speculative risk. You may lose your stake, win a large payout, or in some cases get your money back.

Topic 8: Risk Classification (Part 3): Fundamental vs. Particular Risks

  • Classifying Risk by Cause and Effect

    • While the previous classifications (Financial/Non-Financial and Pure/Speculative) focus on the

      consequences of risk, this third classification looks at the causes and scope of the risk. It distinguishes between risks that affect society as a whole and those that affect individuals. This division gives us two categories: Fundamental Risks and Particular Risks.

  • Fundamental Risk

    • Definition: Fundamental risks are those that affect large groups of individuals, or a large portion of society as a whole. They are impersonal in their origin, meaning they arise from circumstances outside the control of any single individual or group. Their effects are widespread and can impact an entire economy or population.

    • Responsibility: Because these risks are not the fault of any one individual and their impact is societal, dealing with the losses is often considered the responsibility of society as a whole, not just the individuals affected. This is why government-run social insurance programs (like unemployment benefits) often exist to provide coverage against fundamental risks.

    • Examples of Fundamental Risks:

      • Widespread Natural Disasters: Events like major earthquakes, floods, hurricanes, and famines that affect entire regions or countries. The Chernobyl nuclear disaster is another example, with consequences that were felt across multiple countries and still affect agriculture today.

      • General Economic Disasters: Major economic events like high inflation or a severe recession that causes widespread unemployment are fundamental risks. They are caused by broad economic forces, not the actions of one person.

      • Social and Political Upheaval: Events like social revolutions or war are fundamental risks that affect everyone in a society.

    • Insurability: Many fundamental risks are difficult to insure through the private market because their potential for catastrophic, widespread loss could bankrupt an insurance company. However, this is not a strict rule. Some fundamental risks, like earthquakes,

      are covered by private insurance, often with specific conditions and pricing.

  • Particular Risk

    • Definition: Particular risks are, to a large extent, individual in both their origin and their effect. They affect an individual or a small group of individuals, rather than the entire community or society. The cause of the loss is often local and can be identified.

    • Responsibility: The responsibility for managing and bearing the loss from a particular risk typically falls on the individual. Private insurance is the primary mechanism for transferring the financial consequences of these risks.

    • Examples of Particular Risks:

      • Fire: A fire that damages a single home is a particular risk. It affects only that homeowner.

      • Theft: A car being stolen is a particular risk that affects only the car's owner.

      • Disability: An accident that causes a person to become disabled is a particular risk affecting that individual.

    • The Distinction Can Be Subtle: The key difference lies in the impact of the risk. For example:

      • A severe economic recession causing

        widespread unemployment across the country is a fundamental risk.

      • However, for any one individual, the possibility of

        losing their specific job for a particular reason (e.g., company downsizing) is a particular risk. While the general risk of unemployment may be uninsurable by private companies, an individual might be able to purchase insurance to cover their own potential job loss.

Topic 9: Characteristics of an Ideally Insurable Risk (Part 1)

  • Not All Risks Can Be Insured

    • We have now established a clear understanding of risk, recognizing that it broadly involves uncertainty about future losses. Through examining the different categories of risk, it has become apparent that not all risks are insurable. For a private insurance company to be able to accept a risk, the risk must possess a specific set of characteristics. These characteristics ensure that the insurer can predict losses with some accuracy, calculate a fair premium, and remain financially solvent.

  • Characteristic 1: Large Number of Similar Exposure Units

    • This is perhaps the most fundamental requirement. For a risk to be insurable, there must be a

      large number of units that are exposed to a similar risk. These units are often called "exposure units."

    • Why is this important? Insurance operates on the principle of the Law of Large Numbers (which we will discuss in more detail later). By pooling a large number of similar, independent risks, an insurer can predict the number of losses that will occur in a given period with a reasonable degree of accuracy.

    • Example: It is difficult to predict whether one specific house will burn down next year. However, if an insurer covers 500,000 similar houses, they can use statistical data to confidently predict that a certain small percentage of them will have fires. This predictability is essential for calculating premiums. If there are not enough units, predicting losses becomes impossible, and so does calculating a viable premium.

  • Characteristic 2: The Loss Should Be Definite and Measurable

    • The loss, when it occurs, must be

      identifiable and its value must be measurable in financial terms. The insurer needs to be able to determine when a loss has happened and how much it is worth.

    • Why is this important? If a loss cannot be measured, the insurer cannot determine how much to pay in compensation. This is the primary reason why non-financial risks are uninsurable.

    • Example (Measurable): In the case of a car accident, it is easy to determine that a loss has occurred. The extent of the damage to the car can be precisely assessed by a mechanic, who can provide a repair estimate in monetary terms.

    • Example (Not Measurable): It is impossible to insure against a change in a person's psychological state or mood. There is no standard unit of measure by which we can know the "size" of someone's disappointment or anxiety. Therefore, it cannot be insured.

  • Characteristic 3: The Loss Should Be Fortuitous (Accidental)

    • The term "fortuitous" essentially means that the loss must be

      accidental and beyond the control of the insured. The event that causes the loss must be unexpected and happen by chance from the perspective of the person who is insured.

    • Why is this important? Insurance is designed to protect against accidental losses, not intentional ones. If an insured person could deliberately cause a loss and then collect payment, it would lead to rampant fraud and the collapse of the insurance system. This is a critical moral hazard issue.

    • Example (Fortuitous): A lightning strike that causes a house fire is a fortuitous event. The homeowner did not cause it or intend for it to happen.

    • Example (Not Fortuitous): A former employee who was recently fired returns to their old workplace and intentionally sets fire to the building. This is a deliberate, intentional act of arson, not an accidental event. From the insured business owner's perspective the loss was caused intentionally by the employee, however from the perspective of the arsonist, the act was intentional. Insurance would likely not cover this if there was negligence on the part of the insured, but in most cases this is still considered fortuitous from the insured's perspective. The source poses this as a question, but the principle stands that the loss must be accidental

      for the insured. A more clear-cut uninsurable example would be if the business owner themselves set the fire to collect the insurance money.

Topic 10: Characteristics of an Ideally Insurable Risk (Part 2)

  • Continuing the Checklist for Insurability

    • Beyond requiring a large number of units and a measurable, fortuitous loss, several other characteristics must be present for a risk to be considered ideally insurable by a private insurance company.

  • Characteristic 4: The Risk Must Be a Pure Risk

    • As we discussed in our classification of risks, insurance is designed to handle

      pure risks, not speculative risks.

    • Definition Recap: A pure risk is a situation where there are only two possible outcomes: loss or no loss. There is no opportunity for the insured to make a financial gain.

    • Why is this important? The purpose of insurance is to indemnify, which means to restore an insured person to the approximate financial position they were in before the loss occurred. Insuring a speculative risk (which has outcomes of loss, no loss, or gain) would violate this principle. It would be akin to insuring a lottery ticket, where the insurer pays if you lose but you keep the winnings if you win. This would encourage reckless behavior and is unworkable. The Implementing Regulations of the Cooperative Insurance Companies Control Law specifically define "Risk" as a situation with a chance of loss or no loss, but no chance of gain.

  • Characteristic 5: The Loss Should Not Be Catastrophic (Not General)

    • The loss should not be "general," meaning it should not affect a very large number of the insurer's policyholders all at the same time. While an insurer needs a large number of exposure units, it cannot afford for all or most of them to suffer a loss from a single event.

    • Why is this important? The insurance model is based on the principle that only a small fraction of the insured group will suffer a loss at any given time. The premiums from the many pay for the losses of the few. If a single event—like a major war, a massive earthquake in a densely populated city, or a nuclear disaster—causes losses to a huge percentage of policyholders simultaneously, the insurer's pool of funds would be depleted, leading to bankruptcy.

    • Example: An insurer might cover thousands of homes in a specific region. A single house fire is easily payable. However, if a massive hurricane levels the entire region, the insurer would face thousands of total loss claims at once, a catastrophic event that could exceed its financial capacity. This is why some fundamental risks are considered uninsurable by the private market or require special handling through reinsurance and government backstops.

  • Characteristic 6: Insurable Interest Must Exist

    • The principle of insurable interest is a legal cornerstone of insurance. It stipulates that the person applying for insurance (the applicant) must have a

      legally significant interest in the object or person being insured.

    • Definition: This means the applicant must stand to benefit from the continued existence or safety of the insured item and would suffer a genuine financial loss if it were damaged or destroyed.

    • Why is this important? Insurable interest prevents insurance from being used as a gambling contract. It ensures that the person buying the policy is protecting against a real potential loss they would personally suffer, not just wagering on a random event. It also reduces the moral hazard of someone intentionally destroying property they don't own to collect insurance proceeds.

    • Example:

      • You have an insurable interest in your own house, your own car, and your own life. You would suffer a direct financial loss if your house burned down or your car was wrecked.

      • You do not have an insurable interest in your neighbor's house. You cannot buy a fire insurance policy on your neighbor's house because you would not suffer a financial loss if it burned down (and in fact, would stand to gain from the insurance payout, creating a dangerous incentive).

  • Characteristic 7: Premiums Must Be Economically Feasible

    • For the insurance transaction to make sense, the insurer must be able to charge a premium that the insured can afford, while that same premium must be high enough for the insurer to cover all expected losses, pay for its administrative expenses, and realize a sufficient profit.

    • If the likelihood of a loss is too high, the premium required to cover it would become unaffordable for the customer, making the risk uninsurable from a practical, commercial standpoint.

Topic 11: Understanding Uninsurable Risks

  • What Makes a Risk Uninsurable?

    • We have established the key characteristics that a risk must have to be insurable: it must be a pure, fortuitous risk, capable of financial measurement, and part of a large group of similar risks. It logically follows that risks lacking these core characteristics are generally considered

      uninsurable.

    • Primarily, this category includes:

      • Speculative Risks: Risks that offer a chance of gain (e.g., business ventures, stock investments) are not insurable because insurance is not meant to protect against the downside of a gamble while letting the insured keep the upside.

      • Risks Not Capable of Financial Measurement: Risks involving sentimental value or emotional distress cannot be insured because there is no objective way to place a monetary value on the loss.

      • Non-Fortuitous Risks: Losses that are caused intentionally by the insured are not insurable. Insurance protects against accidents, not deliberate acts.

    • It is important to remember that the line between insurable and uninsurable can change over time. As society, technology, and data analysis evolve, what is considered uninsurable today might become insurable tomorrow.

  • Other Factors That Make a Risk Uninsurable

    • Beyond the fundamental characteristics, several other factors can render a risk uninsurable.

  • 1. Lack of Large Numbers

    • The Law of Large Numbers is essential for predicting losses and calculating premiums. If an insurer cannot gather a large number of similar exposure units into a pool, it is impossible to accurately forecast future losses. Without this predictability, a premium cannot be calculated, and the risk cannot be commercially insured.

    • Example: Insuring the launch of a single, unique prototype satellite. There is no large pool of similar satellites to base loss predictions on, making it an extremely difficult risk to insure privately without massive, bespoke premiums.

  • 2. Catastrophic Potential (Fundamental Risks)

    • Certain fundamental risks are uninsurable because their financial consequences are simply too massive for the private insurance industry to handle. A single event could generate so many claims at once that it would bankrupt the entire industry.

    • Examples:

      • War: The level of widespread, intentional destruction caused by war is beyond the capacity of any insurance pool.

      • Nuclear Disasters: The Chernobyl incident is a prime example of a disaster with far-reaching, long-lasting consequences that affected multiple countries and industries, a scale of loss that private insurers cannot cover.

  • 3. Being Against Public Policy (General Interest)

    • Some risks cannot be insured because doing so would be against the "general interest" or "public policy". This means that even if a risk is pure, fortuitous, and measurable, insuring it would be ethically or morally wrong and would harm society.

    • Example: Insuring Against Fines: It is impossible to arrange insurance to pay for fines you might receive for breaking the law (e.g., a speeding ticket or a penalty for smuggling).

      • Why? A fine is a form of punishment designed to deter illegal behavior. If people could buy insurance to pay their fines, the deterrent effect would be lost. It would encourage people to break the law, knowing they wouldn't have to pay the financial penalty themselves. Such an arrangement is clearly against public policy and therefore uninsurable. This also applies to laws in other friendly countries. Another example is a shipment of meat to Saudi Arabia that could not get insurance because the type of meat is not allowed to enter the country.

  • 4. Prohibitively High Premiums

    • In some situations where the likelihood of a loss is extremely high, the risk can become uninsurable for practical economic reasons.

    • Example: Attempting to buy flood insurance for a house that is already built below sea level in an area with no flood defenses. The probability of loss is so high that the technically correct premium would be astronomically expensive, making it completely unaffordable for the homeowner and thus commercially uninsurable.

Topic 12: The Law of Large Numbers: The Statistical Heart of Insurance

  • The Core Principle of Predictability

    • The Law of Large Numbers is a fundamental statistical principle that forms the bedrock of the modern insurance industry. It is the tool that allows insurers to move from uncertainty to predictability.

    • The Law Simply Stated: The greater the number of similar, independent exposure units (e.g., houses, cars, lives) in a group, the more accurately the actual results will approach the expected results. In other words, the

      bigger the sample size, the greater the accuracy of prediction.

  • An Illustrative Example: Tossing a Coin

    • The principle can be easily understood by thinking about a simple coin toss.

      • We know theoretically that the probability of a coin landing on heads or tails is 50/50. This is the

        expected outcome.

      • Small Sample: If you toss a coin just 10 times, it would be very difficult to accurately predict the outcome. It is quite possible you could get 7 heads and 3 tails, or 6 heads and 4 tails, or some other variation. The

        actual result is likely to be far from the 50/50 expectation.

      • Large Sample: If you toss the coin 100,000 times, you can predict with much greater certainty that the outcome will be very close to the 50/50 split, perhaps 55% heads and 45% tails.

      • Even Larger Sample: If you toss the coin 1,000,000 times, the result will get even closer to the expected outcome, perhaps 51% heads and 49% tails. As the number of tosses increases, the gap between the actual result and the expected result shrinks.

  • Applying the Law of Large Numbers to Insurance

    • Insurance companies use this exact principle to do their work. Instead of tossing coins, they are observing losses on insured items.

    • Predicting Future Losses: By insuring a massive number of similar units (e.g., hundreds of thousands of 30-year-old male drivers), an insurer can use past data to predict with high accuracy how many of them will have an accident in the next year. While they can't predict

      which specific person will have an accident, they can predict the total number of accidents for the group.

    • Determining the Degree of Risk: This predictability allows insurers to accurately determine the degree of risk presented by the contributors to the insurance pool.

    • Calculating Fair Premiums: Based on the predicted number and cost of future losses, the insurer can then calculate the correct level of premium that needs to be charged to each member of the pool to cover those losses and the company's expenses.

    • Facilitating Information Sharing: The Law of Large Numbers also helps explain why insurance companies are often willing to share statistical loss data with each other. A larger, shared pool of data benefits everyone in the industry by making their predictions more accurate and their operations more stable.

    • The principle was understood intuitively for centuries. A UK parliamentary legislation in 1601 on marine insurance noted that a loss that would be ruinous for one person becomes manageable when distributed among a large number of individuals, perfectly expressing the rationale of insurance.

Topic 13: Insurance as a Risk Transfer Mechanism

  • The Primary Function of Insurance

    • It is a common misconception to think that insurance prevents or eliminates risk. It does not. Cars will still collide, buildings will still be exposed to fire, and people will still get sick, with or without insurance coverage.

    • The primary function and true role of insurance is to act as a

      risk transfer mechanism. Insurance transfers the financial consequences of a potential loss from one party (the insured) to another, more financially robust party (the insurer or insurance company).

    • The Implementing Regulations in KSA define insurance precisely this way: "transferring the burden of risk from the insured to the insurer and compensating those who suffer from damage or loss by the insurer.”.

  • The Promise of Financial Security

    • When people purchase an insurance policy, they are not buying a physical product. They are buying a

      promise from the insurance company.

    • The promise is that if a specified event (like an accident or a fire) occurs and causes a financial loss, the insured will receive financial compensation to help them recover. If the event does not happen, then no compensation is required.

    • In exchange for this promise, the insured pays a relatively small, known, and fixed amount of money called the

      premium.

    • By paying this small, predictable premium, the insured effectively avoids the possibility of having to bear a much larger, unknown, and potentially financially ruinous loss on their own. This exchange provides peace of mind that arises from financial security.

  • The Core Components of the Transfer Mechanism

    • To successfully facilitate this transfer of risk from the individual to the company, two other crucial functions must be in place: the creation of a common pool and the charging of fair premiums.

  • 1. The Common Insurance Pool

    • Insurers operate by gathering together many parties who all want to share similar types of risks. They establish a

      common pool of funds to pay for the losses that arise from these risks.

    • Insurers receive contributions (premiums) from everyone who wants to join the pool. They place this money into the pool, and it is from this collective fund that they make payments to compensate the few who suffer a covered loss.

    • Therefore, the pool must be large enough to pay for all the losses as well as all the expenses of operating the pool itself.

    • Separate Pools: Insurers do not operate a single giant pool for all risks. A factory owner would not want their premiums used to pay for car accidents, and a car owner would not want to contribute to factory fire losses. Consequently, insurers maintain a series of separate pools—one for motor insurance, one for property insurance, one for marine insurance, and so on.

  • 2. Fair and Equitable Premiums

    • For the pool to operate successfully and fairly, every member must pay a

      fair and reasonable contribution that is proportional to the amount of risk they are transferring into the pool.

    • This contribution (the premium) depends on two main factors:

      • Size of the Risk: This relates to the potential value of the loss. For example, the premium to insure a large, expensive building will be higher than the premium for a small, inexpensive one.

      • Degree of Risk: This relates to the probability of a loss occurring. For example, a car driver with a history of many accidents presents a higher degree of risk and will need to pay a higher premium than a driver with a clean record. Similarly, a house built with superior, fire-resistant materials will have a lower premium than one with inferior construction.

Topic 14: Understanding Perils and Hazards in Risk Analysis

  • Digging Deeper into Risk Analysis

    • We have seen how insurers use the Law of Large Numbers and concepts like frequency and severity to help determine the overall degree of risk in a pool. To analyze and price risk even more accurately for an individual applicant, underwriters must move one step further and distinguish between perils and hazards. These two terms are often used interchangeably in everyday language, but in insurance, they have very distinct meanings.

  • Peril: The Cause of the Loss

    • Definition: A peril is the direct cause of a financial loss. It is the event or phenomenon that brings about the damage. Perils are typically out of the control of any single individual.

    • Examples of Perils:

      • Fire

      • Earthquake

      • Hurricanes

      • Theft

      • Collision

      • Shipwreck

  • Types of Perils in an Insurance Policy

    • Insurance policies are written to respond to specific perils. The policy document will clearly state which perils are covered and which are not. Perils are generally divided into three kinds:

    • 1. Insured Perils (or Named Perils):

      • These are the perils that are

        specifically listed and named in the policy as being covered. If a loss occurs due to one of these named perils, the insurance company is obligated to pay the claim, provided all other policy conditions are met.

      • Example: A property insurance policy states that it covers "loss or damage caused by fire and lightning." In this case, fire and lightning are the insured perils. If the property is damaged by fire, the policy will pay.

    • 2. Excluded Perils:

      • These are the perils that are

        specifically listed and named in the policy as being NOT covered. If a loss occurs due to an excluded peril, the insurance company will not pay the claim. Exclusions are used to eliminate coverage for risks that are uninsurable (like war), are better covered elsewhere, or would require a much higher premium.

      • Example: A policy might cover fire but have an exclusion for "loss or damage caused by explosion". If an explosion destroys the property, the policy will not pay because explosion is an excluded peril.

    • 3. Unnamed Perils:

      • These are any perils that are

        not mentioned at all in the policy—they are neither specifically insured nor specifically excluded.

      • In a standard "Named Perils" policy, if a peril is not named, it is not covered. The burden is on the insured to prove the loss was caused by a named peril.

      • In a broader "All Risks" policy (which is a bit of a misnomer), the opposite is true: everything is covered unless it is specifically excluded. In this case, unnamed perils are generally covered.

Topic 15: The Three Types of Hazards

  • Hazard: A Condition that Increases the Chance of Loss

    • While a peril is the

      cause of a loss, a hazard is any condition or situation that increases the probability (frequency) or the severity of a loss arising from a peril. A hazard doesn't cause the loss itself, but it makes a loss more likely to happen or worse when it does.

  • Example to Differentiate Peril and Hazard:

    • Peril: Collision. This is the direct cause of loss for a car.

    • Hazard: Heavy rain falling on a road. The rain itself doesn't cause the collision, but it creates a condition (poor visibility, slick surface) that increases the chance of a collision occurring.

  • The Three Categories of Hazards

    • Hazards are divided into three distinct kinds: Physical, Moral, and Morale (Attitudinal). It is crucial for an underwriter to identify all three types when assessing a risk.

  • 1. Physical Hazard

    • Definition: A physical hazard is a hazard that arises from the physical characteristics or tangible conditions of the item being insured or its environment. It is something you can see, touch, or measure.

    • Examples of Physical Hazards:

      • For a Building: Poor construction materials, a faulty or outdated electrical wiring system, or being located next to a factory that stores explosive chemicals.

      • For a Car: Worn-out tires, faulty brakes, or driving on a street that is covered in oil.

      • For a Person (Health/Life Insurance): Having a pre-existing medical condition like high blood pressure.

  • 2. Moral Hazard

    • Definition: A moral hazard is a condition that increases the chance of loss arising from the immoral, illegal, or unethical conduct of people. It is related to the character and integrity of the insured person or company's management. It often involves dishonesty and the intention to cause or exaggerate a loss.

    • Moral hazard is much more difficult for an insurer to detect than physical hazard because it is not tangible; it cannot be seen or touched.

    • Examples of Moral Hazard:

      • Dishonesty: An insured person who has a history of fraudulent behavior presents a significant moral hazard.

      • Intentional Acts: The classic example is arson, where a business owner deliberately burns down their own failing business to collect the insurance money.

      • Claim Inflation: People who believe it is acceptable to deliberately inflate an insurance claim are a form of moral hazard. For example, after a minor car accident, they claim for pre-existing damage.

      • Exaggerated Injuries: In liability cases, it is common for claimants to exaggerate the extent of their injuries to get a larger settlement. This is often supported by sympathetic doctors or lawyers, increasing the cost of claims for the insurer.

  • 3. Morale Hazard (Attitudinal Hazard)

    • Definition: Morale hazard (also called attitudinal hazard) describes an increase in risk that arises from an insured's indifference, carelessness, or irresponsible attitude towards loss, precisely because they know they have insurance coverage.

    • Distinction from Moral Hazard: This is different from moral hazard because there is no conscious or malicious intent to cause a loss. The person is not trying to cheat the insurance company. They are simply less careful than they would be if they had to bear the full cost of a loss themselves.

    • Morale hazard acts to increase both the frequency and the severity of losses.

    • Examples of Morale Hazard:

      • Leaving a car unlocked with the keys in the ignition because "it's insured against theft anyway."

      • Failing to clear ice from the walkway in front of a shop, knowing that if someone slips and falls, the liability insurance will cover it.

      • A company with a poor safety culture where employees are unorganized, messy, ignore "No Smoking" signs, and do not follow correct safety procedures. Their careless attitude has increased the risk of a fire or accident peril occurring.

Topic 16: An Introduction to Reinsurance

  • What is Reinsurance? The Concept of "Insurance for Insurers"

    • Reinsurance is a critical process in the global insurance market. In simple terms, reinsurance is

      insurance for insurance companies.

    • It is a technical process where an insurance company (known as the "insurer" or the "ceding company") transfers all or part of the liabilities it has accepted from its policyholders to another company, known as the

      reinsurer.

    • In exchange for taking on this risk, the insurer pays a fee or premium to the reinsurer.

    • The KSA Implementing Regulations define a reinsurer as "an insurance or reinsurance company that accepts insurance contracts from another insurer". The regulations define the act of reinsurance as the "Transfer of the insured’s risk from the insurer to the reinsurer" where the reinsurer agrees to indemnify (compensate) the insurer for payments it makes to its policyholders.

  • The Chain of Contracts

    • It is crucial to understand the contractual relationships in a reinsurance arrangement. There are two separate contracts:

      1. The Original Insurance Contract: This is between the original policyholder (the insured) and their insurance company (the insurer).

      2. The Reinsurance Contract: This is between the insurance company (now acting as the ceding party) and the reinsurance company (the reinsurer).

    • No Direct Relationship: There is absolutely no legal or contractual relationship between the original insured and the reinsurer. The original insured is often not even aware that their policy has been reinsured.

    • Example of Financial Consequences: If a reinsurer were to go into liquidation and become unable to pay claims, the financial problem would fall squarely on the insurance company, not the original policyholder. The insurer is still 100% responsible for paying the policyholder's claim according to the original insurance contract. The insurer would then suffer an unrecoverable loss from the failed reinsurer.

  • How Reinsurance Works in Practice

    • Imagine a client offers an insurance company a very large risk to insure—for example, a massive petrochemical plant worth billions of dollars.

    • The insurer might consider the risk too large or too hazardous for it to accept on its own. A single major loss at the plant could threaten the insurer's financial stability.

    • The insurer has two choices:

      1. Reject the Business: The insurer could simply refuse to provide the insurance. However, this has disadvantages. The insurer would lose the premium income and potentially damage its relationship with a valuable client or broker, who might then take all their other business elsewhere.

      2. Accept and Reinsure: A better option is for the insurer to accept the risk and then arrange to transfer a portion of that risk to one or more reinsurers. This allows the insurer to safely take on the business, serve its client, and earn a premium, while protecting itself from a catastrophic loss.

Topic 17: Key Reasons Why Insurers Use Reinsurance

  • Beyond Single Large Risks

    • While reinsurance is essential for handling very large individual risks, insurance companies arrange reinsurance for a variety of strategic, financial, and commercial reasons. It is a fundamental tool for managing an insurer's overall portfolio and financial health. Insurers are guardians of the funds belonging to all their policyholders, and reinsurance is a key way they safeguard that pool of money.

  • 1. To Increase Capacity

    • Definition: Every insurance company has a limited financial capacity, which is the maximum amount of risk it can safely accept for its own account. This capacity is determined by its capital and surplus.

    • Function: Reinsurance helps an insurer dramatically increase its underwriting capacity. It allows the insurer to write policies with limits far higher than what it could safely retain on its own.

    • Example: An insurer might have a policy to retain a maximum of $1 million on any single property. By using reinsurance, that same insurer can confidently issue a policy for a $50 million factory, knowing that it can transfer $49 million of the risk to its reinsurers. This makes the insurer more competitive and able to service large corporate clients.

  • 2. To Provide Stability and Smooth Underwriting Results

    • Definition: An insurer's financial results can fluctuate wildly from year to year due to the unpredictable nature of losses. A year with few large losses will be very profitable, while a year with several major disasters could be devastating.

    • Function: Reinsurance helps to stabilize these results and avoid extreme fluctuations in the cost of claims. It smooths out the peaks and valleys, leading to more predictable and stable year-over-year financial performance.

    • Example: By arranging a type of reinsurance that pays for all losses above a certain large amount (e.g., $5 million), an insurer protects itself from the shock of a single catastrophic event. This ensures that one disastrous event doesn't wipe out the year's profits or jeopardize the common pool. This helps keep the loss ratio (claims divided by premium) stable.

  • 3. To Obtain Disaster or Catastrophe Protection

    • Definition: This is a specialized form of stability, focusing on protecting the insurer from the immense financial impact of a single, large-scale catastrophic event.

    • Function: When an insurer suffers huge losses from a major event like a hurricane, earthquake, or industrial explosion, it can face severe financial problems. Catastrophe reinsurance is designed to help the insurer avoid such problems by having the reinsurer pay for the bulk of the losses once they exceed a very high threshold.

    • Example: An insurer in Florida knows it is exposed to hurricanes. It might buy catastrophe reinsurance that kicks in only after its total losses from a single hurricane exceed $100 million. This protects the company from insolvency in the event of a truly massive storm.

  • 4. To Apportion and Diversify Risks Globally

    • Function: Reinsurance acts as a mechanism for spreading risk not just among companies, but across the entire globe. It takes the financial burden of a loss that might be concentrated in one country's economy and distributes it to reinsurers located in various regions of the world.

    • Example: A major earthquake in Japan could cause hundreds of billions of dollars in losses. If this was all borne by Japanese insurance companies, it could cripple their economy. Through reinsurance, a large portion of that loss is transferred to reinsurers in London, Bermuda, Germany, Switzerland, and the United States, spreading the financial impact globally.

  • 5. To Gain Peace of Mind

    • Function: Just as an individual policyholder buys insurance to gain peace of mind about their home or business, an insurance company buys reinsurance for the same reason. The management and shareholders of the insurance company want to be confident that one single bad event will not jeopardize the entire company and the funds it holds for its other policyholders. Reinsurance provides this essential comfort and security.

Topic 18: The Main Types of Reinsurance: Facultative vs. Treaty

  • Two Fundamental Approaches to Arranging Reinsurance

    • When an insurance company decides to transfer risk to a reinsurer, the transaction can be structured in one of two main ways: Facultative Reinsurance or Treaty Reinsurance. The choice between them depends on the nature of the risk, the frequency of transactions, and the insurer's strategic needs.

  • A. Facultative Reinsurance ("Optional" or "Case-by-Case")

    • Definition: The word "facultative" is of French origin and means "optional". This perfectly describes the nature of this reinsurance. It is an

      optional, case-by-case method where each individual risk is offered by the insurer to a reinsurer, and the reinsurer has the freedom and choice to either accept it or reject it.

    • The Process:

      1. The insurance company has a specific risk it wants to reinsure (e.g., a large factory).

      2. It must contact a reinsurer and provide all the substantial facts and details about that individual risk.

      3. The reinsurer analyzes the risk and decides whether to accept it. They can also negotiate the terms, such as the premium, the amount of risk they will cover, and any special conditions.

      4. If the reinsurer rejects the risk or its proposed terms are unacceptable, the insurer must start the process all over again and try to find another reinsurer.

    • Advantages: It is considered one of the best methods for very large, unusual, or highly specialized risks (like aviation insurance) where a tailored solution is needed.

    • Disadvantages: This process is very time-consuming and has a high administrative cost. Most importantly, it creates

      uncertainty for the insurance company. The insurer doesn't know if it can secure reinsurance, or at what price, until after it has already accepted the risk from the client. These delays and uncertainties can cause significant problems.

  • B. Treaty Reinsurance ("Automatic" or "Obligatory")

    • Definition: Treaty reinsurance is a pre-negotiated agreement (or "treaty") between an insurer and a reinsurer. Under this agreement, the insurer is obligated to cede (transfer) all risks that fall within the treaty's terms, and the

      reinsurer is obligated to accept them automatically. The reinsurer agrees in advance to accept a whole class or portfolio of risks.

    • The Process:

      1. The insurer and reinsurer negotiate and sign a treaty, which is typically valid for one year and can be renewed. The treaty will specify the limits, the types of risks covered (e.g., "all motor policies written in Saudi Arabia"), and the percentage or amount of risk to be transferred.

      2. Once the treaty is in place, whenever the insurer writes a new policy that fits the treaty's criteria, that policy is automatically reinsured without any further negotiation.

    • Example of Obligation: An insurer has a treaty with a reinsurer where the reinsurer agrees to accept 25% of all fire insurance policies issued by the insurer. If the insurer then accepts a new fire policy, the reinsurer cannot refuse to accept its 25% share, even if it has concerns about a specific condition of that policy, because it is obligated by the treaty.

    • Advantages: The main benefit for the insurer is certainty and efficiency. The insurer knows in advance that it has reinsurance protection in place and it knows exactly what the cost of that protection will be as soon as it accepts a new policy from a customer. This eliminates the delays and uncertainty associated with the facultative method.

Topic 19: Proportional Reinsurance: Sharing Risks and Premiums

  • A Deeper Dive into Treaty Reinsurance

    • Treaty reinsurance agreements, which provide automatic coverage for a portfolio of risks, can be broadly divided into two main categories based on how the risk is shared: Proportional Reinsurance and Non-Proportional Reinsurance. This page will focus on the proportional method.

  • Proportional Reinsurance (Pro Rata)

    • Definition: Proportional reinsurance is a type of treaty where the insurer (the ceding company) and the reinsurer agree to share the risks, the premiums, and the claims on a pre-agreed percentage basis. The KSA Implementing Regulations define it as a treaty where the insurer commits to assign a certain percentage of its risks, and the reinsurer commits to accept them. If the reinsurer takes on 40% of the risk, it will receive 40% of the premium and pay 40% of any losses.

    • There are two main types of proportional reinsurance agreements: Quota Share and Surplus Share.

  • 1. Quota Share Agreements

    • Definition: Under a quota share agreement, the insurance company agrees to cede a fixed, predetermined percentage of every single insurance policy it writes within a specific branch or department (e.g., all motor policies) to the reinsurer. The reinsurer is obligated to accept this share of every policy.

    • In return, the reinsurer receives that same percentage of the total premiums for all transactions covered by the agreement. When a loss occurs, the reinsurer pays its corresponding percentage of the damages.

    • Example:

      • An insurer has a 70% Quota Share treaty with a reinsurer for its car insurance portfolio. The insurer keeps 30% of every risk for itself (this is called its "retention").

      • The insurer writes a policy for a car with a sum insured of SAR 100,000 and a premium of SAR 3,000.

      • The car has an accident, and the loss amount is SAR 50,000.

      • How it is shared:

        • Sum Insured: Insurer retains SAR 30,000 (30%); Reinsurer's share is SAR 70,000 (70%).

        • Premium: Insurer keeps SAR 900 (30%); Reinsurer receives SAR 2,100 (70%).

        • Loss: Insurer pays SAR 15,000 (30%); Reinsurer pays SAR 35,000 (70%).

  • 2. Surplus Share Agreements

    • Definition: A surplus share agreement is more selective than a quota share. The insurance company first decides on a specific monetary amount of risk it is willing to retain for its own account on any single risk. This amount is called the

      "retention limit" or "line". The insurer then cedes the "surplus" amount—the part of the sum insured that exceeds its retention—to the reinsurer, up to an agreed maximum.

    • If a policy's sum insured is less than or equal to the retention limit, the insurer keeps 100% of the risk and premium, and the reinsurer is not involved at all.

    • The capacity of a surplus treaty is expressed in terms of "lines." For example, a "5-line" treaty means the reinsurer can accept a surplus amount up to 5 times the insurer's retention limit.

    • Example:

      • An insurer has a Surplus Share treaty with a retention limit of SAR 1,000,000 per risk. The treaty has a capacity of 4 lines, meaning the reinsurer can take on a surplus of up to SAR 4,000,000. The total capacity for a single risk is SAR 5,000,000 (1 line for the insurer + 4 lines for the reinsurer).

      • A policy is written with a sum insured of

        SAR 4,000,000, a premium of SAR 100,000, and a loss of SAR 800,000 occurs.

      • How it is shared:

        • Risk Distribution: The insurer retains its limit of SAR 1,000,000. The remaining SAR 3,000,000 is the surplus ceded to the reinsurer.

        • Percentage Share: The insurer's share is 1M / 4M = 25%. The reinsurer's share is 3M / 4M =

          75%.

        • Premium: Insurer keeps SAR 25,000 (25%); Reinsurer receives SAR 75,000 (75%).

        • Loss: Insurer pays SAR 200,000 (25%); Reinsurer pays SAR 600,000 (75%).

Topic 20: Non-Proportional Reinsurance: Focusing on Loss Severity

  • An Alternative to Proportional Sharing

    • We have explored proportional reinsurance, where the insurer and reinsurer share risks, premiums, and losses based on a fixed percentage. The second main category of treaty reinsurance operates on a completely different principle: Non-Proportional Reinsurance, also known as "Excess of Loss" reinsurance.

  • Non-Proportional Reinsurance (Excess of Loss)

    • Definition: This type of reinsurance is based on the severity of losses, not on sharing the sums insured or premiums. The insurance company determines its share by deciding on the maximum amount of loss it is willing to bear from any single event or from an accumulation of events. This amount is called the "deductible" or "retention". The reinsurance company then agrees to pay for any loss amount that

      exceeds that agreed-upon limit, up to a specified maximum.

    • Because this agreement does not depend on a fixed percentage for distributing sums insured and premiums, it is called "non-proportional". The premium paid to the reinsurer is not a direct percentage of the original premiums; instead, it is a calculated fee designed to cover the expected losses and expenses for the excess layer of coverage.

    • The KSA Implementing Regulations define it as a treaty where the insurer is responsible for losses up to a certain amount, and the reinsurer commits to accept the risk that exceeds that amount.

  • How It Works: The "Excess of" Principle

    • The core idea is that the reinsurer only gets involved when a loss is large enough to breach the insurer's retention. For small and medium-sized losses, the insurer bears the entire cost.

    • Example 1: A Single Large Loss

      • An insurance company buys a reinsurance policy with a limit of "SAR 15 Million excess of SAR 10 Million."

      • This means the insurer will pay 100% of any loss up to SAR 10 million.

      • The reinsurer will pay the portion of the loss that exceeds SAR 10 million, up to a maximum reinsurance payment of SAR 15 million.

      • This covers total losses up to SAR 25 million (10M insurer + 15M reinsurer).

      • Scenario A: Loss of SAR 8 Million. The loss is less than the SAR 10 million retention. The insurer pays the full SAR 8 million. The reinsurer pays nothing.

      • Scenario B: Loss of SAR 25 Million. This is a large loss. The insurer pays the first SAR 10 million (its retention). The

        reinsurer pays the remaining SAR 15 million (the excess amount).

      • Scenario C: Loss of SAR 30 Million. A catastrophic loss. The insurer pays the first SAR 10 million. The reinsurer pays its full limit of SAR 15 million. The final SAR 5 million is not covered by this specific reinsurance layer and remains the responsibility of the insurer (unless it has purchased another, higher layer of reinsurance).

  • Key Differences from Proportional Reinsurance

    • Focus: Proportional reinsurance focuses on sharing the entire risk from the first dollar. Non-proportional reinsurance focuses only on the severity of the loss.

    • Premium Sharing: In proportional treaties, premiums are shared by a fixed percentage. In non-proportional treaties, the reinsurance premium is a calculated fee that bears no direct percentage relationship to the underlying original premiums.

    • Loss Sharing: In proportional treaties, every loss, no matter how small, is shared. In non-proportional treaties, the reinsurer pays nothing unless the loss is large enough to exceed the insurer's retention.

Topic 21: Co-insurance and Self-Insurance: Alternative Risk Strategies

  • Handling Risks Beyond Reinsurance

    • When an insurer is faced with a risk that is too large or too hazardous for it to accept entirely on its own, reinsurance is the most common solution. However, there are other strategies that can be used to manage risk, both by insurers and by the businesses seeking coverage. Two important alternatives are co-insurance and self-insurance.

  • Co-insurance: Sharing a Risk Among Multiple Insurers

    • Definition: Instead of one insurer accepting 100% of a large risk and then using reinsurance to transfer a portion of it, the risk can be shared directly among two or more separate insurance companies from the start. This practice is known as co-insurance, and the participating insurers are called co-insurers.

    • Each co-insurer accepts a specific percentage of the total risk and is in a direct contractual relationship with the insured for that percentage only.

    • The Process: Co-insurance is common for insuring very large risks and is often arranged by an insurance broker.

      1. The broker's primary responsibility is to ensure the entire risk is covered 100%, not leaving their client partially uninsured.

      2. The broker will approach an insurer who they think will be willing to be the

        "lead insurer".

      3. The lead insurer assesses the risk, determines the premium rate and all other terms and conditions, and may conduct a survey of the property. The leader then agrees to take a certain percentage of the risk (e.g., 50%).

      4. The broker then approaches other insurers, who must decide if they are willing to "follow" the terms set by the lead company and take on their own percentage of the risk.

      5. The broker continues this process until 100% of the risk is placed.

    • Example of Financial Consequences:

      • A large property is insured via co-insurance: Insurer A (Lead) has 50%, Insurer B has 25%, Insurer C has 15%, and Insurer D has 10%.

      • If Insurer C goes into liquidation and a total loss occurs, the insured can only collect 85% of their loss from the remaining three insurers (A, B, and D). The final 15% is an unrecoverable loss for the insured, who must pursue the liquidator of Insurer C for their share. The other co-insurers have no obligation to cover the failed insurer's portion.

  • Self-Insurance: Deliberately Retaining Risk

    • Definition: Self-insurance is a deliberate and conscious decision by a person or business to retain a risk rather than transferring it to an insurance company.

    • Distinction from "No Insurance": This should not be confused with having no insurance. "No insurance" occurs when a person simply ignores a risk and has made no plan to pay for any losses that may occur. Self-insurance, in contrast, is a formal risk management strategy where a business acknowledges a risk and makes a plan to fund potential losses itself.

    • The KSA Implementing Regulations define it as "the allocation of a regular balance, to meet the expected losses of the risk to be insured instead of serving the company”.

    • When is it used? A business might choose to self-insure a risk that it considers to be small and well within its financial ability to manage. This is common for risks that are low severity/low frequency, or even high frequency if the losses are small and geographically spread out.

    • Example: A large retail clothing chain has 250 shops across the kingdom. Each shop has a large plate glass window at the front, which would cost about SAR 5,000 to replace if broken.

      • The company might choose to

        self-insure this risk. Instead of paying an insurance premium, they might put the equivalent amount of money aside into a dedicated fund. When a window breaks at any of their stores, they use the money from this fund to pay for the replacement.

      • Why? They believe they can save on the insurer's administrative costs and profit margins built into the premium. They also have a large number of similar risks (250 windows) spread out geographically, which makes their potential losses fairly predictable, much like an insurance company. The funds set aside can also be invested to generate a return.

Topic 22: The Broad Economic and Social Benefits of Insurance

  • More Than Just Paying Claims

    • The primary function of insurance is to transfer risk and provide compensation for losses. However, the existence of a robust and healthy insurance industry provides numerous additional benefits to individuals, businesses, and society as a whole.

  • A. Provides Peace of Mind

    • This is the principal benefit for the individual policyholder. By paying a relatively small, known premium, the policyholder is comforted by the knowledge that if a disaster should occur—like a fire destroying their home or business—financial compensation will be available to help them rebuild and recover. This exchange of a large, unknown potential expense for a small, known one provides invaluable financial security and reduces anxiety.

  • B. Encourages Risk Reduction and Loss Prevention

    • Insurance companies have a vested financial interest in reducing the number and severity of claims. Therefore, they often combine their resources to invest considerable sums of money in loss prevention research and activities.

    • This includes testing new methods of fire detection and firefighting equipment, crash-testing cars to improve safety, and researching the use of less flammable materials in consumer goods.

    • They share this knowledge with their policyholders, advising them on how to minimize their risks, which leads to lower claims costs and, in turn, lower premiums for everyone. This has the added societal benefit of fewer accidents, less personal suffering, and reduced loss of economic output.

  • C. Avoids the Need for Capital Retention

    • If insurance were not available, businesses would have to set aside large amounts of their own capital as a contingency fund "just in case" a major loss occurred.

    • Insurance frees up this capital. Instead of having money sitting idle in a contingency fund, a business can pay a small premium and then more advantageously use its capital to invest in expansion, innovation, and growth.

  • D. Encourages New Enterprises and Investment

    • Starting a new business requires significant capital investment, often raised from banks or investors. These investors typically require the business's assets (like buildings and machinery) to be used as security for the loan or investment.

    • Investors would be very reluctant to provide this capital if insurance was not available to protect these assets. A single fire could easily wipe out the assets and make the business unprofitable. By insuring assets against perils like fire, insurance provides critical protection for investors, thereby encouraging investment and helping new enterprises to get started and continue operating.

  • E. Serves as a Major Source of Investment Capital

    • As custodians of the insurance pool, insurers have vast amounts of money under their care. There is a time lag between when premiums are received and when claims are paid out, which can be many years in the case of life assurance policies.

    • These funds are not left idle; they are invested in a wide range of areas, including direct equity investments in companies, loans to industry and governments, and property development. The small premiums paid by thousands of individuals are pooled together and circulated back into the economy, helping to stimulate national growth.

  • F. Contributes to Foreign Exchange and International Trade

    • Insurance is an intangible commodity that is traded between countries, just like physical goods. When a country's insurance industry sells coverage to an overseas entity, it is "exporting" an invisible service and earning foreign currency. Conversely, a country that must buy its insurance from overseas is an "importer" of insurance services.

    • Having a strong, financially sound domestic insurance industry that can retain its own risks helps a country reduce the amount of foreign currency it has to spend on importing insurance, which is valuable for its balance of payments.

  • G. Creates Job Opportunities

    • A successful insurance industry is a significant source of employment. It creates job opportunities not only directly within insurance and reinsurance companies, but also in a vast ecosystem of related professions and services. This includes roles for brokers, loss adjusters, risk managers, and service providers like hospitals, medical centers, car repair shops, and companies that provide safety and security equipment.
